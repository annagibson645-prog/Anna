Two things he says about the set, and both matter:

Every mārga is hard. He does not rank them by difficulty and then recommend the easy one. Every mārga is very hard to connect to the supreme. What determines whether you arrive is not which path you took but the way you do it — the ārādhana performed in the infinity of complete present awareness.

And you can merge all five. It is not the case that only one type of sādhana or one type of pūjā must be performed. You may merge all five faces, all five tattva guṇas, and become completely the seeker of the creator.

Which sets up the correction he returns to at the end of that talk: having done your ārādhana in the dakṣiṇa mārga, do not then say vāmācāra is wrong. Do you think Śiva split himself into five faces so that one would be good and another wrong?

6. Karma

He spends roughly ten minutes of the talk on this, which makes it the single largest block in the episode. It is not an aside. In the ārādhana of Kāla Bhairava, karma is the subject.

Where this comes from

This is the largest single block in the Kāla Bhairava talk — roughly ten minutes of it — and it is delivered as a transmission: Bhairava teaches it to Viṣṇu at Vaikuṇṭha, and Viṣṇu passes it to humankind. The narrative of that episode stays in chapter 9; the teaching itself is here, because it governs all ten chapters and not one.

Why it came from that Bhairava. Brahmā’s ego is removed by a fingernail. Viṣṇu’s is not. Viṣṇu carried an equal share of the same pride, but nothing is severed from him — instead he is made to articulate what karma is, and in the articulating, the pride dissolves. Two removals, two methods. One surgical; the other the older and stranger technique of being compelled to explain the thing you had forgotten applied to you.

And why karma belongs to Kāla in particular. Karma unfolds only in time. Kāla is time, and kāla is Bhairava himself. He is not one force among others adjusting your karma. He is the medium it moves through. That connection should be made here and paid off in chapter 9.

The four lamps

Before the categories, the ritual, because the ritual is the argument in physical form.

In the pūjā of Bhairava, four dīpas are lit. The four lamps are the four karmas. The gesture is stated plainly: these are the four karmas which, as a human being, I have brought here. Please burn them.

Not four lamps for auspiciousness. Four lamps because there are four things you arrived carrying, and this is what you do with them.

1. Sañcita — the warehouse

The accumulated store. Every karma from every prior life, held.

He is emphatic about the scale, and impatient with people who underestimate it: you may think one to ten — no. Lakhs of janmas. Countless lives, and the residue of all of them in storage.

His metaphor: the archer’s quiver. The box holding the arrows is sañcita. Not the arrows in flight, not the one currently drawn — the container, and everything still in it.

What makes the image good is what it withholds. The archer knows what is in the quiver. You do not. You never see the store. You only ever meet the single arrow currently in the air.

Example. You meet someone and there is immediate, unaccountable recognition — or immediate aversion, with nothing in this life to explain either. You inherit a fear you were never taught. A place you have never been feels like return. Nothing in the present accounts for these, and sañcita is the name for the account they are drawn on.

The purpose of the human birth. He attaches a large claim here: after lakhs of janmas we arrive in the human form so that we may come to know the consciousness of the deva who created us. The human birth is not incidental in this scheme. It is the birth in which the store can finally be addressed rather than merely extended.

2. Prārabdha — the arrow already shot

The portion of sañcita drawn out to bear fruit in this life.

His accounting: of 999 in the warehouse, nine are brought forward so that those nine can be cleared. The balance sheet settled, one page at a time.

And it is the same arrow, now released. Prārabdha is the arrow taken from the quiver, set to the bow, and shot. It cannot be recalled. That is the whole force of the image — not that it is painful, but that it is past the point of decision.

What prārabdha fixes: - the current birth - the family - the body - the lifespan — the total span carried - the major events of the life

Example. The body you woke up in. The family you were born to. The country, the century, the language you think in. The illness that runs in the line. None of it chosen, none of it negotiable, all of it already in flight before you had an opinion.

This is what people mean when they say it is my prārabdha that this happened, or it is my prārabdha that I am connected to you. Not fatalism — accounting.

Worth noting (inference, not his statement — confirm): the arrow’s flight is fixed; the stance of the one it reaches may not be. Prārabdha appears to govern what arrives, while āgāmi governs what you make of the arrival. He does not say this outright, but the four categories only cohere if something like it is true.

3. Āgāmi — what is being made now

The karma being generated in this present life, including everything done in unawareness.

Thoughts. Words. Actions. Deeds. And here is the clause that catches people:

Thought alone creates karma.

I have not hurt anyone outside me is not a defence. The thought I need to go and hit him, the intention to make trouble for someone — that is already āgāmi karma, generated and filed, whether or not a hand was ever raised.

Example. The argument rehearsed in the shower with someone who is not there. The resentment maintained daily for years at no visible cost. The email composed and never sent. On this account none of that is neutral, and none of it is private.

On mistakes. He draws a line that is easy to miss: the human capacity is to recognise I have made a mistake — and once it is understood, that should be the last of it. Not endless repetition followed by endless remorse. Understanding is meant to terminate the pattern, not accompany it.

“Your child is your karma.” What comes of the union of masculine and feminine is not simply happiness. It is a teaching in how conscious you are required to become. The example arrives with a will of its own and does not stop instructing.

When it lands: later in this life, or in a future birth. This is the category with the longest fuse.

The connection to practice. This is what the 112 ways of the Vijñāna Bhairava Tantra are pointed at — not exotic technique, but how consciously you are living. Thoughts, emotions, actions: be conscious of these three, and the karma being generated comes out appropriate rather than accidental.

4. Kriyamāṇa — instant

Immediate karma. The same lifetime, often the same hour.

His example, and he notes the world already has a name for it: a man does something wrong, walks off down the road, scratches a car, and a hundred metres later he falls. People call it instant karma. That is precisely kriyamāṇa.

Example. The sharp remark that costs you the relationship the same afternoon. The shortcut taken at work that surfaces before the week is out. The lie told at breakfast that has collapsed by dinner.

And the clause that makes this the hopeful category: kriyamāṇa is powerful enough to influence, and even cancel, future events belonging to the other karmas.

Which means present action is not only debt service. What you do now can reach forward and alter what is queued. Of the four, this is the one still fully in your hands — and he says outright that this is the one to be most conscious of.

The four qualities of karma

A second fourfold scheme, and a different axis. Not four kinds of karma, but four flavours any karma can carry. He moves between the two schemes with only a light signal, so they are easy to conflate.

Śukla — white

Born of sāttvika. The day you feel well, and well-connected.

What defines it is not pleasantness but knowing your own action. I am eating consciously. I am picking this up deliberately. I know what is to be done. Not “it happened accidentally.”

Every human carries all three shades. Śukla is the sāttvika one surfacing.

Kṛṣṇa — black

Arises where tāmasika and rājasika combine. Inertia joined to agitation.

And the clarification that matters most in the whole section: Kṛṣṇa here is not the name of the god. Kṛṣṇa is a guṇa. Kṛṣṇa is a consciousness — the meeting point of those two. This is why Kālī belongs here, and why one of her names is Kṛṣṇa Kālī.

What it covers: selfishness, violence, ignorance.

And the Kṛṣṇa avatāra is the demonstration rather than the exception — demons destroyed, violence performed, and throughout it all remaining taṭastha, neutral, unattached to the doing. The point being that violence is not the sole measure of an act. The karma is.

Śukla-Kṛṣṇa — mixed

Partly good, partly bad. Most human action lives here, and he says so without disapproval — it is simply the ordinary condition.

The image: the half-black, half-white figure the monks of Japan and China use — white consciousness and black consciousness merged. That merging is the human thought process.

And the calendar carries it: śukla pakṣa and kṛṣṇa pakṣa, fifteen days each, every month, forever alternating.

Akarma — action in pure awareness

Performed with no attachment to the fruit. This is karma yoga, and it is the only one of the four qualities that is a destination rather than a description.

He is harshest here, and the target is the transactional saṅkalpa:

I will do three maṇḍalas, 144 days. And once it is done I will shave my head — or give a thousand rupees to a poor man, or feed the street dogs, or give dāna to a beggar. And you give me this.

Who are you trying to fool? When even the creators made the mistake, he did not let them off. You are offering a bribe. And what such a saṅkalpa actually manufactures is more śukla-kṛṣṇa karma — the very mixture you were trying to escape.

Offering from surrender, or from love, is a different act entirely. The naivedya is not a payment.

What akarma sounds like instead:

This janma was given by you. I am simply sitting in front of you, doing my ārādhana. You know better than I do what I need — what has to be removed from this life, what has to be given. You know better than my little logical mind.

How Bhairava interacts with all of it

  • He attends to the four layers of karma-phala. This is given as the reason he enters a life at all. He always comes to teach something, and what he handles is the karma.
  • He is the medium, not an actor upon it. Karma unfolds only in time. Kāla is time. Kāla is Bhairava himself — which is what the name is doing. He does not adjust your karma from outside it.
  • Surrender dissolves karmic bondage. He removed it for Brahmā and for Viṣṇu. We are a speck in front of him, imagining we are driving all these karmas. Nothing is in our hands.
  • The four lamps are the mechanism in ritual form. The four karmas laid down and burnt.
  • The 48-day sādhana spans both pakṣas — kṛṣṇa and śukla — so that the black and the white karma born of the three guṇas are both covered within a single month.
  • The 144 days are the threshold, and nothing substitutes. Someone reporting six hundred days, ten maṇḍalas, and a list of unresolved problems has been keeping a checklist, not doing a sādhana. In sādhana you forget yourself — including the count.
  • The GPS. He can tell you the destination, the roads, the potholes, the closed lanes. He cannot make the journey. It is only you who reaches.

Where karma stops

Not in better karma. Not in accumulating enough white to outweigh the black.

Karma stops when one realises the self — the ātman — beyond doership.

And he specifies: not the mind. The ātman. Once you identify what is actually driving all of this, that is the thing which never dies — and the one that was never the doer to begin with.

This is where the whole subchapter has been going. The four karmas are an accounting system. The four qualities are a description of the ledger’s contents. Neither is the exit. The exit is discovering there was no accountholder.

9. You do not repress desire — you offer it

Desire is not escaped. To escape is only to postpone.

It is offered, and in the offering it is transmuted — into divine will, into divine love and bliss. The divine is not bondage. It is the fire that leads to freedom.

10. The feminine — Śakti, and how women are regarded

This belongs in Foundations because it is a rule governing the whole practice, not a feature of one Bhairava. It sits here, immediately before the ten, deliberately: the reader should meet it before meeting any of them.

Every woman is Mā svarūpiṇī

In Kaula tantra it is not only about the masculine or the feminine — but the greatest importance is given to the feminine energy. In Kaula cosmology the feminine is the topmost consideration, held more auspicious even than the masculine.

This is why she is addressed as Mā. Not as an honorific for particular women, and not as a metaphor. Every woman is Mā svarūpiṇī — the Devī in form.

And the rule holds without exception

This is the part that has teeth, because it is stated in the terms most likely to be used to exclude someone.

Even if she has not bathed. Even if she is in the middle of her menstrual cycle. Even if she is not in a state conventionally considered clean enough to perform pūjā — in Kaula tantra she is still of the highest purity, and still the most auspicious.

Note what that does. Every ordinary ground for treating a woman as temporarily unfit is named and then overruled. The purity is not a status she can lose. It is what she is.

Twelve days

A woman performing 12 days of sādhana is held equivalent to 30 or 33 days performed by a male sādhaka.

He introduces this expecting it to be startling — maybe you would be shocked to hear this — and offers no hedge. Nearly a factor of three.

[ASK GURU] — the textual basis for this, and whether the figure is 30 or 33. He gives both in the same breath. Worth having the source before it goes in print, because it is the kind of claim a reader will want to check.

The obligation on the sādhaka

The rule is not descriptive. It is binding on practice:

The one who gives the highest respect to the feminine is the true follower of the corresponding Śakti.

And the sharper form: Bhairava himself is considered of highest prominence only when he bows down to her.

The instruction in the Kāmeśvara talk is the same, put practically — as Bhairava sādhakas we must give the highest respect to women, recognising her as none other than Mā Kāmeśvarī herself, and going before her: Mā, whatever is essential according to dharma, please take that action.

You cannot worship Śiva alone

This is the structural claim underneath all of the above, and it is not sentiment.

In Kaula tantra, if you perform the ārādhana of Śiva alone, it is incomplete. Unless you merge Śiva with Śakti, liberation — the dissolution of illusion — does not come into existence at all.

And the reciprocal is stated just as flatly: without Śiva, Śakti’s energy is nothing. The energy can be there, but without Śiva she has no svarūpa. Which is the same proposition the Kāmeśvara chapter opens with — Śiva without Śakti is a corpse; Śakti without Śiva is movement without a base.

So the respect is not an ethical add-on to the practice. It is the practice. A sādhana that treats the feminine as secondary is not a sādhana that has been performed badly. It is one that structurally cannot complete.

12. Reading is not enough

Placed last in Foundations, immediately before the ten, because it governs how everything after it should be read.

A lifetime for one syllable

He is unambiguous about the timescale, and he attributes it to the scriptures and to his own guru:

A lifetime is required to activate even one syllable of a mantra — or even one Bhairava svarūpa.

And the target of this is the crash course. Social media will tell you that in 3 hours, 3 days, 7 days or 11 days you can experience the whole of the Daśa. His response: I bow down to the people who say such things. What such a course actually gives is an experience for a time limit. Unless it becomes your sādhana, it is never experienced at all.

[ASK GURU] / [EXPAND] — how to present this without crushing the reader. A book that opens with this takes a lifetime and you will probably not finish loses people on page one. But softening it would be the exact dishonesty he is objecting to. See Open Questions — this needs a framing, and the framing should come from him rather than from us.

This path is not gentle — it forges you

From the Kabandha / Chinnamastā talk, but stated by the guru as true of every Bhairava path, not only this one:

Once a sādhaka steps onto this path, it is transformative, not gentle. You will be buried every minute, because you need to become much more potent and energetic — to be able to hold the energy you are calling down. Like gold, unless it has been heated and tempered, the glitter never comes: the Devī will temper the sādhaka with more karmas, cleansing all of them, so the sādhaka can transform. This brings freedom through a sudden lightning bolt of awareness, rather than gradual change — and it is never a gentle transformation. Never think that in the comfort of your sādhanā you will be able to transform; it is never possible.

This connects directly to readiness — adhikāra — which cannot be faked, and to the two sections below on why people stop.

The people who stop, and why

He lists the reasons plainly, and they are not dramatic ones:

  • I don’t have the time.
  • I have a different priority.
  • I have already read this.

The third is the one this section exists for.

Reading is a concept. Understanding and experiencing with bhāva is what carries you to liberation.

So: the book you are holding is not the practice. It can carry you to understanding. It cannot carry you to experience, and it is not a substitute for either a guru or the days.

14. Bhairava’s Iconography

From the āyudhas talk — general to Bhairava, not tied to one of the ten svarūpas, so placed here in Foundations. [EXPAND — the other nine āyudhas, plus the vāhana, still need drafting; see the cleaned transcript.] Headings in this section are provisional and will likely change as it fills in.

What an āyudha is

  • Not a weapon — an instrument of power. The Sanskrit word āyudha does not simply mean “weapon.” Many people conflate the two, and that conflation changes the meaning entirely. An āyudha is an instrument of power: divine consciousness performing a specific function.
  • Each āyudha corresponds to a cosmic principle. Each āyudha a Bhairava svarūpa holds carries a psychological process, and each carries a spiritual transformation. It is a responsibility for the sādhaka to know what exactly the svarūpa is holding, and why.

The triśūla

  • Three prongs, three parts of the sādhaka. The triśūla represents identifying the sādhaka — three different identities, three different parts of the sādhaka: one prong is ego, one prong is ignorance, one prong is attachment.
  • It is about conquering yourself. The triśūla is not asking the sādhaka to conquer others; it is not a showpiece to show off or to conquer others. It is asking the sādhaka to conquer himself first. The real triśūla must first arise within.
  • The social-media problem. People use the triśūla on social media, but often without the internal change that would make it mean anything — it becomes a decoration rather than a responsibility. [Anna: this is very visible in aghorī-style videos on YouTube, and in the wave of Hanuman / akhāḍā-style workout content — a lot of it has the strength but lacks the spiritual dimension the āyudha requires, so it’s rendered impotent, because it isn’t approached with the right attitude.]
  • A second meaning: past, present, and future. On the outer layer it may look like a three-pointed spear, but the three points also symbolise the sādhaka’s past, present, and future. Most people suffer because the mind constantly moves between regret over the past and anxiety about the future. The triśūla teaches becoming established in the eternal present: Bhairava stands beyond time, untouched by past or future.
  • The four states, and turīya. Consciousness exists as four states: waking, dreaming, deep sleep, and turīya, the fourth state, which transcends the three guṇas (sāttvika, rājasika, tāmasika) that the rest of the universe functions through. Turīya avasthā can only be reached in the present moment; becoming established in that eternal presence is what it truly means to be in the now.
  • The responsibility this asks of the sādhaka. Stop running through memories and expectations, and enter direct awareness. The true triśūla is not the metal; it is freedom from psychological time — past and future are always psychological time. This minute is what is truly in hand.

The khaḍga

  • Viveka — spiritual discrimination. The outer meaning of the khaḍga is the blade: chopping off, capturing someone’s head, cutting someone off. Its innermost meaning is viveka, spiritual discrimination — being in presence, without even discriminating what is good, bad, or ugly; the ability to separate truth from illusion.
  • What the sword cuts. The sword cuts false belief. It cuts emotional confusion. It cuts egoic identity. It cuts superstition. It cuts spiritual fantasy.
  • The story of Brahmā. When Bhairava chopped off Brahmā’s head, he was very clear — he had viveka at that moment, the spiritual discrimination, very clear about separating truth from illusion, because Brahmā was carrying the illusion. [EXPAND / ASK GURU — the full story of why Brahmā’s head was chopped off isn’t told in this talk; it’s only referenced as the proof-case for viveka. Needs its own telling, probably here or in Part One §2, “Who is Bhairava.”]

The kapāla

  • Profound liberation — mokṣa. The kapāla, the skull bowl, is perhaps Bhairava’s most misunderstood symbol. People associate it with fear, with death, with occult practice. Its meaning is profoundly not that: it means a profound liberation. [“Attaining mokṣa” is Anna’s gloss; the guru’s own word in this talk is “liberation.”] Liberation from the inevitable fate of everybody — the king and the beggar become equal when they die, when the agni burns their body on the corpse. The skull declares very clearly that everything you identify with is temporary.
  • Death is a continuous occurring. Bhairava holds the kapāla to remind the sādhaka that death is not a future event. Everyone thinks someone else dies — a neighbour, an uncle, a relative — and that they themselves never die. But every breath is teaching you that you are nearing death. Every birthday you celebrate tells you that you are near to death. Every day and night, every meal, every moment: you should be aware you are heading towards it. Because it is always happening to someone, it is happening continuously, and Bhairava carries it to remind the sādhaka.
  • The kapāla teaches surrender. The responsibility of the sādhaka is remembering impermanence consistently — not to become depressed, but to become free. Not to hold on to something, not to live into the future or the past, but to hold this moment. Life is short and temporary, and this is why practice is necessary.

The ḍamaru

  • The heartbeat of creation. There is no difference between Śiva and Bhairava; Śiva, in a particular tattva, becomes Bhairava. The ḍamaru is the heartbeat of creation. He is the creator: as Pañcavaktra, he created the entire cosmos — Brahmā, Viṣṇu, all the lokādhipatis; he is the adhipati of every sṛṣṭi. (This is spoken about further in the Pañcavaktra chapter.)
  • The principle of vibration. Before form even existed, vibration existed; before that vibration, pure awareness had to exist. The ḍamaru symbolises the cosmic sound, a sacred vibration, and is the origin of language — not the words we speak, but vibration itself as language. [ASK GURU — Anna wants to know whether this vibration principle also equates to Skanda. See Open Questions.]
  • Every mantra originates from the primal vibration. The sādhaka’s responsibility is to become aware of each sound, even the subtle sound the ears cannot hear, and to eventually discover the inner nāda: the sound that exists before all sound existed. The ḍamaru is the origin of creation.

The pāśa [EXPAND]

  • Not what it first looks like. The pāśa is a noose. At first it reads as threatening — you’d think it means Bhairava will kill you, or that, like the kapāla, it represents impermanence. That is not really the case. It functions more like a lasso, a rope that pulls you toward liberation. Yama also carries one: when his subordinates come to take a soul, they put the pāśa on the person, pick him up, and take him to whatever loka he needs to go to, usually Yamapurī. In this form, Bhairava holds it.
  • What it represents. It represents everything that binds human consciousness. He pulls out desire, fear, addiction, attachment, and identification.
  • Anna’s note — it binds you to the path. [Personal reflection, not from the guru’s talk — ask Guruji whether it’s okay to include.] The pāśa also serves to lasso the sādhaka to the path, and to bind them to it. It is very difficult to leave once you have started, because a sādhaka takes a saṅkalpa to work with Bhairava. Once he has you, he has you — you can’t really leave him. Whether in this life or the next, you will encounter him again.

The aṅkuśa

  • Immense power, without guidance. The elephant possesses immense power — its energy, its Śakti, is so capable it can break or do anything, the most powerful being in existence. Yet without guidance it can become very destructive. The aṅkuśa is what captures, or holds, that immense power.
  • What it symbolises: the mind. The aṅkuśa symbolises the mind — very powerful, very intelligent, unpredictable. It represents guidance, not suppression: that powerful mind can help the sādhaka become guided, and the intelligence can be directed toward spiritual growth. Bhairava does not destroy the mind; he retains it.
  • Anna’s note — guided by dharma. [Personal reflection — ask Guruji whether it’s okay to include, per the Open Question above.] Guided in the right direction by dharma, that power turns into something like a weapon. Without guidance, with all that power — that Śakti — coursing through and enhancing whatever is already going on in a person’s life, the mind becomes destructive, distracted.
  • Mastery is intelligent direction, not control. Control is not suppression. Mastery is intelligent direction: rather than controlling the mind, make it guidance. Don’t take the mind toward unpredictability — take that as mastery.

The daṇḍa

  • Spiritual authority. The staff, the daṇḍa, represents, or symbolises, spiritual authority.
  • Self-discipline — specifically spiritual self-discipline. Without discipline, spiritual power becomes very dangerous. This is what the daṇḍa is teaching.
  • Anna’s note — timing and dispersal. [Personal reflection — ask Guruji whether it’s okay to include, per the Open Question above.] If a sādhaka lets discipline slip — doing the sādhana at a different time each day rather than holding to a fixed one — any Śakti being cultivated on this path gets dispersed, or used poorly, misapplied. Not that the Śakti gained is meant to be applied outward; the point is that inconsistency itself disperses it.

The bells

  • Around the waist — a call to awakening. Bhairava wears the bells around his waist. Whenever they sound, the sound pierces through the metal noise, but every ring carries a silent message for the sādhaka: return to awareness. Whatever the sādhaka started, return back to it.
  • Bringing the sādhaka’s mind back during sādhana. The bells are what bring the sādhaka’s mind back to him during sādhana. [Anna: this is what he is doing when you read it in the Kāla Bhairava Aṣṭakam — he is asking, in effect, “come back, come back to awareness of me.” Confirm the exact verse/reference with the guru.]
  • Why this matters. The mind consistently drifts between one thought and another, one action and another. The bell interrupts that drift — that unconsciousness — and the sādhaka needs to remain very alert. This is why he is visualised with the bells around his waist: spiritual life begins when there is attention, and when the sādhaka holds that attention, he is with Bhairava.

The fire

  • Nothing enters the fire and remains unchanged. Sādhanā is about transformation. Whatever enters the fire has to change.
  • What it burns away — from the sādhaka’s life:
    • It will tear the sādhaka apart from what he is not.
    • It will squeeze him if he tries to do certain dramas.
    • It will remove him from everything.
    • It will detach him from his own loved ones.
    • It will detach him from his actions.
    • It will detach him from his [unclear: “sraaya”].
    • It will detach him from his guru.
    • If he is not fit, the fire will burn him — and if he is still sitting there afterward, only then is he a sādhaka.
  • Bhairava is about the fire — what it burns:
    • He burns ego.
    • He first burns fear.
    • He burns karma.
    • He burns ignorance.
    • He burns limitation — the limitation of the sādhaka’s knowledge, the limitation of his action, the limitation of the burden he carries, whatever he thinks is “mine.”
    This is why Bhairava is feared.

Bhairava chooses you

[Not tied to a single āyudha — this is general to Bhairava, from the same talk. Placed here for now; may belong instead in Part One §2, “Who is Bhairava.”]

  • Anna’s note — like dating, except reversed. [Personal reflection — ask Guruji whether it’s okay to include, per the Open Question above.] Worshipping God in Hinduism is often like dating: you gradually get to know the person, you may spend years courting them, trying to get them to notice you, before it becomes something like a marriage. With Bhairava it runs the other way. He already knows, and he pulls the sādhaka to him — the sādhaka does not go to him first.
  • You do not choose Bhairava — he chooses you. He brings the sādhaka to him, because of countless promises, saṅkalpas, and offerings already made — prayers the sādhaka does not even remember making. This is not only from this lifetime; it is across many lifetimes, simply forgotten. Bhairava has to call the sādhaka back to the path.
  • The path he calls you back to is not an easy one. Coming to Bhairava means losing everything that is false, and everything that is not true.

The serpent (nāga)

  • Śakti and awakening, not danger. The serpent represents awakening power, Śakti. Many think it stands for danger — it is not a danger, not an evil. [Anna: this is unusual in metaphysical thought, especially among monotheistic religions, where the serpent could represent something like danger instead. Worth naming as a point of contrast — ask Guruji if he wants this comparison drawn out, or leave it as Anna’s observation.]
  • Why the nāga: eternal vigilance and cyclical renewal. Bhairava holds the nāga because it activates kuṇḍalinī, and that is an awareness — an eternal vigilance which is needed, a cyclical renewal.
  • Shedding skin, shedding identity. The nāga sheds its skin repeatedly — it is always shedding what it doesn’t need. Likewise the sādhaka must continuously shed his old identity: keep evolving, and never become attached to who you think you are.

The vāhana — Śvāna (the dog)

  • Śvāna — “dog” in Sanskrit. Kāla Bhairava’s vāhana is the dog, śvāna.
  • Loyalty. The dog represents loyalty: to dharma, to sādhanā, to pūjā vidhāna, to guru, to oneself. If a sādhaka betrays even one of these, he is not a sādhaka at all.
  • Anna’s note — loyalty, truth, and Vāk Siddhi. [Personal reflection — ask Guruji whether it’s okay to include, per the Open Question above; also confirm the Vāk Siddhi connection, which isn’t stated in this talk.] Loyalty and truth are extremely important: if a sādhaka lies too often, the sādhanā will not go well, and Vāk Siddhi will not be attained. Loyalty is extremely essential in Bhairava sādhanā, and the dog is the hint of this.
  • Alertness. The dog is most alert even in sleep — a call to spiritual awareness. It also represents fearlessness under all circumstances: the ability to hold, and not crumble, when dealing with something difficult.

Awareness — the final weapon

  • The story. A sādhaka once asked Bhairava, after his maṇḍala sādhanā, which of his weapons was the most powerful. Bhairava replied: the weapon that destroys ignorance. The sādhaka guessed the triśūla — Bhairava smiled and said no. He guessed the khaḍga — Bhairava smiled again and said no. Confused, the sādhaka finally received the answer: Bhairava touched the sādhaka’s forehead and told him the most powerful weapon is awareness. When awareness is awakened, every other weapon has completed its work.
  • What this means for the sādhaka. The highest Bhairava sādhaka is not about holding or showcasing a weapon — it is caitanya, awareness, that is truly important. The āyudha exists only until this awakening happens; it exists only to destroy the āsuric nature. Once the truth is realised, the battlefield automatically disappears. Fear, ego, resentment, lethargic behaviour — this is the real battlefield the sādhaka fights with all these āyudhas. When it is won, the warrior disappears: the sādhaka disappears, and becomes Bhairava.
  • The goal of every āyudha. The goal of the āyudha is to become worthy of it. Once that worthiness awakens, every āyudha is no longer needed in the same way. Make yourself disciplined, make yourself aware — that is what connects the sādhaka to Bhairava tattva.

Stories and mythological events

  • How Akṣobhya came into being: the quarrel of the elemental gods. (Akṣobhya transcript, [6:52]–[10:58].)
    • The gods grow proud. In the early days, the ṛṣis and munis did tapas to particular gods — Agni Deva, Vāyu Deva, Indra Deva, the elemental gods — and gained siddhis from them. In time the elemental gods became proud and glorious, and began to argue among themselves over which of them was the highest. Indra thundered that he, the owner of the thunder, was the most powerful. Vāyu howled that he was much more powerful. Agni burned and declared, “I am the most powerful in the entire cosmos.”
    • The cosmos falls out of balance. The ṛṣis no longer knew which devatā to activate to keep the cosmos in balance. The celestial world filled with fear, and imbalance began to rumble. The Saptarṣis went directly to Mahādeva and asked Guru Bhairava: “Show us the truth. Whose ārādhana must we do so that the entire cosmos can be balanced?”
    • A dark form emerges — from silence. In response, from the silent heart of Paramaśiva, a dark form began to emerge. He was not roaring like a Rudra. He was not dancing like Naṭarāja. He simply stood — with a smile, silent, unmoved, very powerful. He carried no weapons. He did not argue, did not declare himself, did not take part in the quarrel at all. He only stood, and watched.
    • They looked into his eyes. The gods looked into his eyes and saw no ego, no anger, no pride — only a vast stillness, the kind of stillness that makes the mind blow away. He was born not from fire but from silence: a silent fire, arising not from wrath but from the awareness of Paramaśiva.
    • One look, and the pride shattered. He looked at the gods, and their pride shattered. Their minds grew calm and their hearts softened. Without a word of argument, the question of who was most powerful was settled. [Anna’s note: the gods themselves recognised him as the most powerful, and she reads that as coming from exactly this — he did not engage, he simply stood in silence and watched. The talk shows the recognition happening through his gaze and stillness; why that makes him so powerful is put to Guruji in Open Questions.]
    • His teaching to the devagaṇa. Akṣobhya taught them: “The real storm is not outside you. It is inside your mind. Be still, be centered, be free” — free from all these egoic practices.
  • Devānanda, and Tārā Mā in the cremation ground. (Akṣobhya transcript, [33:41]–[40:52].) The guru tells this story to show the connection between Mā Tārā and Akṣobhya Bhairava.
    • The sādhaka. Once there was an upāsaka named Devānanda [name as written in “that particular tantric book,” per the guru], doing the sādhana of Devī Tārā Mā in the Tāraka forest, near the river Tāraka [place names as heard — confirm]. He was very simple and poor in wealth: no money, no fame, no name. But he was rich in his love towards Mā, very rich in his inner connection.
    • His practice. Every night he would sit near the cremation ground and softly speak one particular Tārā Mā mantra — three bīja mantras with phaṭ. [The guru does not recite the mantra; he says it cannot be recited here.] With full faith he offered her only blue flowers, and only in his heart: not physically, but mentally, emotionally.
    • The warning. People who saw him used to mock him. Even the priest told him: “Please don’t do this. Mahā Tārā is very dangerous. She is a very fiery goddess; she is very furious. Do not call upon her without a proper guru, or your life will be terrible.” Devānanda always replied: “She is my mother. And what would a mother do to her child?”
    • The tantric. One night, dark clouds filled the sky, and a tantric came into the village with evil power. He began stealing the people’s peace, their wealth, and their dreams, and fear spread until the whole village was in turbulence. He challenged the villagers: “Let your god protect you.” He too was a sādhaka of Tārā Mā — but he had activated his tāmasika and rājasika guṇa, and his ego was at its peak: “I am a guru. I can do anything. You need only listen to me.” [The guru adds that Devī and Bhairava can be activated in whatever svarūpa — sāttvika, tāmasika or rājasika — depending on each upāsaka’s will and wish.]
    • His call. Devānanda ran to the cremation ground and called to Mā: “Mā, O Mā, save us from this turbulence of the tantric who has come and is challenging us. If I have ever called you in love, please hear me now, and please come and support.” He performed no physical ritual. Only with mānasika tapas he offered blue flowers at her lotus feet, calling on her with the bījākṣara mantra, recited in the mind with full love.
    • Tārā appears. Suddenly, like a fire of thunder, in a blue lightning, Mā Tārā appeared: dark, in the dark of the night, wild hair flying, so beautiful, with the radiance of thousands of suns. She held the khaḍga and kapāla, and her third eye was burning in the truth. She walked to the tantric, looked deep into his soul, and said: “You challenged dharma with ego, but I came here with love, in truth.”
    • The spell broken. With one roar she shattered the spell and freed the man [possibly “the land” — caption unclear], and all the people of the village were freed. She turned to Devānanda and smiled like a gentle mother: “You called me with bhāva, not with fear. I always come to my children whenever they call with love.” Then she disappeared into the cremation smoke.
    • What it teaches. Mā Tārā does not appear to ritual alone, but to pure devotion. For those who surrender in love, she becomes both protector and liberator. Siddhis may come, but her complete invocation — bringing her fully into your life — is only possible with love. Whenever you call Devī or Bhairava, call with love. [39:27]–[40:52]
  • Ṛṣi Kaśyapa and Mahāmāyā. (Akṣobhya transcript, [41:00]–[44:46].) The guru tells this as the Akṣobhya story that runs alongside the Devānanda story. [Name as heard, “Kashapan”; read as Kaśyapa — confirm with the guru, since a Kaśyapa also appears in the Ekabhatra and Kāmeśvara chapters.]
    • The ṛṣi and his goal. In the eastern mountains there was a ṛṣi called Kaśyapa, performing the deepest tapas.   • Two kalaśas, four arms. In his particular svarūpa he performs abhiṣeka to himself: four arms, and in two of them he holds kalaśas, performing self-abhiṣeka. (See Stories and mythological events, below, for what this means and what the kalaśa contains.)
  • Mṛgaśīrṣa mudrā — the gaze of the deer. He is associated with mṛgaśīrṣa mudrā, the gaze of the deer. The fullness of innocence and self-realisation is what the two kalaśas represent altogether.
  • He is the ātmaliṅga. Tryambaka Bhairava is the ātmaliṅga spoken of in the Śaivite Purāṇas. All of this happens once Śakti, the knowledge, awakens within.

Stories and mythological events

  • Who anoints the anointer? During the early age of creation, a question arose among the devatās and siddhas: who anoints the anointer? Who performs the pūjā of the one who is beyond pūjā? The sages asked Brahmā, and this is where the mystery of the kalaśa was disclosed — the kalaśa within which Bhairava resides, while most forms of Śiva instead receive the abhiṣeka from others.
  • Why self-abhiṣeka, when that isn’t typically a thing. In most svarūpas of Śiva, the abhiṣeka is performed to him. Tryambaka Bhairava is unique: he performs the abhiṣeka on himself, using both kalaśas he holds in his four arms. When the ṛṣis asked what this meant, Brahmā decoded it for them: this is not an act of ego, but ānanda — pure joy, the purest form of joy, the overflowing bliss of awareness, where Śiva himself performs the kalaśa pūjā of his own self. This is recorded in Rudrālaya Tantra and Kāśī Khaṇḍa.
  • What is in the kalaśa: three aspects. Per Kāśī Khaṇḍa, the kalaśa contains three things:
    • Amṛta — the nectar of immortality: the essence of eternal life, timeless consciousness, consciousness itself.
    • Bindutva — the consecrated essence of creation, the seed of all manifestation held within Bhairava. (Connected to bindu-tattva as it appears in Maṇidvīpa in the Devī Purāṇa, and in the Meru Parvata / Śrī Yantra perspective.)
    • Caitanya — pure consciousness, in a liquid form: consciousness as it appears in air, in liquid, in the pañcabhūtas.
  • Devī Bhuvaneśvarī’s question, and his answer. Bhuvaneśvarī questioned his stillness — the way he was still, the way he had isolated himself, experiencing self at the highest level. She asked him: “Do you not seek offerings? Do you not seek any praise? How, then, are you still fully active and available?” He was able to convey this to her very simply, with a smile: his own kalaśa consists of two eternal ingredients, and pouring amṛta over his own head is why he is always in a blissful state. He claims, in effect: “I am that which gives to itself.” Offering to self is not selfish — there is a thin line between selfishness and selflessness, and once you know yourself, you become selfless. He tells her: “I am that which gives to itself. I am the joy unto myself. This is the abhiṣeka of the undivided one within me.”
  • Duryodhana’s failure to realise. Duryodhana had Kṛṣṇa in front of him, in svarūpa — Kṛṣṇa showed him his avatāra before showing it to Arjuna — and Duryodhana was not able to realise him at all. He could not know that this was the Śiva svarūpa, the Bhairava svarūpa, Viṣṇu himself, the creator. He never realised this, because he failed to understand that the divine is complete in itself, that it needs no offering — that it is “I am what gives to itself.” Only once you give time to yourself can you realise the external creations of the creator; unless you experience the tattva, you will not experience the real essence of the creator.

Even Atri’s strength and Śakti was none other than Mā Anasūyā. She is one of the greatest tapasvinīs; through her devotion she was able to bring all the devatās to her. She is the mother of Dattātreya Svāmī, whose three heads represent Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Maheśvara, and so she is called the mother of Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Maheśvara.

    • The story.   • The silence of Ṛṣi Bharadvāja. (From the second Tryambaka Bhairava talk.)
    • Who he was. Another great seeker, in the lineage of ṛṣis that comes after Atri Maharṣi. The story is set in one of the yugas, like Aruṇa yuga, in the highest Himālayan realms. Bharadvāja was immersed in intense penance, trying to understand the Śivatva inside him.
    • What he was not seeking. He did not want wealth, fame, or siddhis. The guru contrasts this with sādhakas today who talk about the deva while grasping at fame, or who expect to gain or achieve something from their practice. Sādhana, he says, is everything about losing yourself, and Śivatva is none other than losing yourself.

    • The knowledge stayed out of reach. Despite all his sincere effort, the knowledge he sought remained just out of reach. As in every such story, the guru says, God will test us and break us, but never leave us.
    • What the Vedas whispered. Only one who has burned the desire for knowledge may truly see the fire. This broke something in Bharadvāja: he realised he was still wanting. Even wanting wisdom, even wanting what is sacred, is a longing that puts distance between you and Śiva.
    • His vow. He resolved that if the truth would not reveal itself, his body would die in that silence. He took the oath and turned to mauna — not mauna as we usually mean it, not just not speaking — for 21 nights.
    • The twenty-first night. In the darkest night, the brightest light suddenly filled the cave where he sat in tapas. Guru Bhairava came, as Tryambaka, radiant, his third eye flaming. Each of his eyes held one of the three sacred fires Bharadvāja had longed for.

These three fires are living within you. Outer penance and fasting can cleanse the body, but not the mind and ego; for that, the sādhana and the fire have to come from inside.
    • The triśūla strikes. Bhairava asked him why he was stuck in the practical world, wanting something that was already inside him, and struck the earth with his triśūla. The cave shook, and — not externally, but as an inner cave — a cave of light appeared. Bharadvāja came to know perfect stillness, and through it what Śiva is, what Paramaśivatva is, what Bhairava consciousness is.

Each of the three fires did its work in him, and his remaining desire to know came to an end — not through anything superstitious, but through Bhairava’s transformation, his triśūla struck inside him. Guru teachings

  • Śiva is inside you. Where is Śiva? Śiva is inside you. Śiva tattva is inside you. Unless you know the Śiva tattva inside you, external pūjā or external rituals are always in vain, or a mechanical performance of an activity. As Bhairava sādhakas, understanding Bhairava and experiencing Bhairava, within yourself, is much more important than external rituals — this matters especially when it comes to worshipping Tryambaka Bhairava.
  • You become part of Bhairava consciousness only when you realise you are his guṇa. When you understand, “I am Bhairava, I am the guṇa of Bhairava,” only then do you become part of Bhairava consciousness.
  • Why self-abhiṣeka: the divine is complete in itself. Śiva doesn’t require milk. Śiva doesn’t require any kind of pūjā, havana, or yajña — any external offering at all. This is why he performs the abhiṣeka on himself.
  • Once you start tantra, understanding yourself comes first. Once you start the tantra, once you are in the tantric rituals — whichever guṇa, sāttvika, tāmasika, or rājasika — self-realisation, understanding the self tattva, is the most important, the key aspect.

Description

  • A hidden — gupta — devatā. The fourth of the Daśa Mahā Bhairavas, and very rarely spoken about. The guru calls his svarūpa “really dormant”: many have not spoken about it, and many do not know it at all. ([Confirm with the guru] “gupta” is our word, not his; his own words are “dormant” and “very rarely spoken about.”)

Historical veracity

Stories and mythological events

  • The story of Parvata Rāja and Dhūmāvatī, told in full:
    • Source: the Pramodā Dhūmāvatī Tantra, described as one of the lesser-known episodes in that text
    • Who Parvata Rāja is: not a person in the ordinary sense — “Parvata Rāja” refers to the mountains themselves, personified as a king. At the time of this story, the mountains were governed/“owned” by King Parvata — a divine being who had performed great penance in order to witness the first form of goodness
    • Setup — why mountains, specifically: it’s said that any sādhaka seeking deep sādhanā ascends to higher elevations, because consciousness itself is associated with Sahasrāra, not Mūlādhāra. Mūlādhāra grounds us in the practical world; Sahasrāra is consciousness elevated to its highest peak — the peak from which the greatest penance, and the first appearance of Devī, becomes possible. This is the frame the whole story sits inside.
    • The event itself: while Parvata sat in meditation, offering himself in devotion, trying to raise his consciousness to the next level, the sky itself began to roar — because Dhūmāvatī’s essence, in her Dhūma form, was active and laughing at that exact moment, as Parvata tried to meditate
    • The obstacle, explained directly: she is smoke — and once that kind of smoke rises unaddressed, you remain fixed in Mūlādhāra. This is precisely where Dhūmāvata, together with Dhūmāvatī, appeared: laughing, as smoke, evolving into the mountain itself
    • The physical trace, still claimed today: if you look at those mountains now, they are surrounded by smoke — the Pramodā Dhūmāvatī Tantra states plainly that this smoke is him: visible, yet fundamentally unknown to the one who sees it
    • The cosmic merging: as Dhūmāvata evolved into the mountain as smoke, Dhūma appeared in the clouds as a flame while Parvata continued his sādhanā, and Dhūmāvatī appeared alongside him, in the very frame of the mountain
    • Her blessing — and its meaning: she offered her blessing not as a widow, but as “queen of the hidden flames” — meaning the hidden flames within every sādhaka, every person in existence. Everyone carries something hidden that has never been disclosed to anyone — a hidden smoke, unknown even to the one carrying it. This is exactly what she demonstrates by bestowing this role on Parvata.
    • Her words to him directly, worth quoting closely rather than paraphrasing further: “Wherever your smoke arises from the depth of your heart, there I shall dwell. I will come and reside there, and make sure that whatever is unknown within that smoke becomes clear. But it is Dhūmāvata whose fire must be active first. Unless you activate Dhūmāvata — the one who reveals the Creator’s creation, the unknown within the known — your connection to your penance, to your sādhanā, will always weaken.”
    • Why this matters beyond Parvata specifically: whenever anyone undertakes any sādhanā, something is always hidden — you may be occupied with something else entirely, but something inside you is quietly on fire regardless. This is why Dhūmāvatī comes not only to Parvata but to all her sādhakas: she is ready to clear every hidden flame, every rising smoke — but Dhūmāvata, the fire itself, has to be activated first.
    • What Parvata actually does in response: leaves comfort, goes to the cremation ground, performs intense inner sādhanā. This is what leads him to Śābara Vidyā — tantric knowledge offered specifically to both Dhūmāvatī and Dhūmāvata, where the two reside together.
    • The resolution: through Śābara Vidyā, Parvata attains enlightenment and “becomes subtle.” There are accounts — even in the Śiva Purāṇa — describing mountains that used to walk, used to move; his very form changed after this episode. Dhūmāvatī made him realize his inner fire could only be dissolved with Dhūmāvata’s help; once that inner smoke transformed into knowledge, he became settled — which, in this telling, is how mountains became fixed in place.
    • The meaning, stated directly rather than left implicit — do not present this as a literal geological claim: don’t take this as though mountains once physically walked. In Sanātana Dharma, the five elements are understood as a gift to humankind, both outside and within us, making us aware that we are part of nature. Logic serves practical purposes; this teaching is explicitly framed as operating above ordinary logic. The mountain settling is the story’s image for a sādhaka’s own inner fire settling — restlessness (the “walking” mountain, the unaddressed smoke) resolving into stillness once Dhūmāvata is properly activated and the hidden inner fire becomes knowledge rather than a source of continued unrest.

Historical veracity

  • Gorakhnāth established Bhairava-pīṭhas specifically for the Ekavaktra svarūpa in Nepal, Assam, and Gujarat — belonging to the Ekavaktra tradition within Kaula Yoga
  • Not confined to the South — despite Nātha/Kāya Yoga practice being more commonly associated with South India, this tradition’s pīṭhas exist across Nepal, Assam, and Gujarat as well
  • Why it isn’t more widely known: it’s Gupta — deliberately hidden, kept secret. This isn’t an accident of history; it’s intentional. It was never meant to be publicly taught or discussed openly.
  • Practical warning worth stating plainly in the book: genuine teaching in this specific tradition is extremely rare to find today. If someone is found publicly teaching or selling access to it — especially for money — that is a strong signal it’s a scam, not the real transmission. Authentic transmission doesn’t work that way.

Stories and mythological events

  • Source traditions, named directly: the Kaula Tantra and Kashmir Shaivism

    • Kashmir Shaivism is noted as preserving very little written material specifically on this svarūpa
    • the Kaula Tantra and the broader Mahāvidyā traditions carry most of what’s covered here

  • Story 1 — Rishi Kashyapa (his first invocation):

    • set during an asuric age when yajña had stopped and even rishis had ceased pūjā to the devatās
    • Kashyapa performed a year of tapas on the banks of the Sarasvatī, seeking to stabilize the chaos and restore ritual serenity
    • Bhairava appeared in a vision under a full moon and, when asked to disclose himself, gave the name Yajñeśvara — not “Bhairava,” not “Ekavaktra”
    • revealed himself as the force that makes a yajña fruitful not through fire, but through the inner silence of the one performing the sacrifice
    • Kashyapa’s offering — yellow cloth, ghee, turmeric flowers, given in silence — is described as the first known invocation/pūjā of this svarūpa

  • Story 2 — Rishi Dadhīchi (the Vajra story), told in full:

    • Dadhīchi introduced as a rishi of unshakable tapasyā
    • Indra, king of the devas, needs a weapon against the asuric forces and approaches Dadhīchi wanting to forge the Vajra — Indra’s own thunderbolt-weapon — from his bones
    • Dadhīchi initially refuses to give up his life
    • he’s told what the sacrifice actually requires: to give up his body, have his bones converted into the āyudha (weapon), and — critically — to meet death in complete stillness, without regret, not simply die
    • he accepts the process, but is left wondering, genuinely, how he could actually attain that stillness — acceptance alone doesn’t get him there
    • during his sādhanā, he invokes Bhairava — through Rishi Bhṛgu, specifically via the ādharaṇa-mantra
    • Bhṛgu invokes through the Vedas the same way Kashyapa did earlier in Story 1 — this is presented as the same lineage of invocation, not a separate method
    • twelve sacred mantras belonging to Yajñeśvara exist within the Veda itself — Bhṛgu recites all twelve to help Dadhīchi
    • Bhairava/Yajñeśvara is activated on the ninth day of the tapas, specifically in the cremation ground
    • why the cremation ground, again: stated directly as “the place where everything ends and everything starts” — same justification as elsewhere in this chapter and in the Mātaṅga chapter
    • the moment of appearance: wind stops, everything stops, golden silence — he appears as a golden flame, one face, eyes half-closed
    • Dadhīchi speaks to him directly: “With Bhṛgu’s help, I understand that after reciting these twelve mantras, I will be able to invoke you, O Guru Bhairava. How can I take this life away such that I can become an āyudha to the devaloka?”
    • Yajñeśvara’s answer isn’t just permission — he grants Dadhīchi the stillness of the sky itself, teaching him how to attain and maintain that stillness, so the offering could be made through mantra-power rather than through flesh
    • a specific mantra paired with a mudrā is given — the mudrā brings all five senses under control, producing complete stillness; the talk notes this same mudrā can bring anyone into that state, not just Dadhīchi
    • the death itself becomes “a living yajña” — not an ending performed as ritual, but the ritual itself, happening in real time: his body dissolves, his consciousness merges into Bhairava-consciousness
    • result: his bones become the āyudha for the devaloka, accepted by Indra — this is, per the talk, how the Vajra came to exist
    • coda tying it forward: the Vajra’s power recurs later in mythology — when Devī confronts Śumbha and Niśumbha, all the devatās arm her with their own weapons, and Indra specifically gives her this same Vajra
    • guru’s own framing for telling this story at all: not really about Dadhīchi or the Vajra as the point — it’s offered as evidence of how deeply Guru Bhairava is “connected to so many articulations of the mythological points.” Worth deciding whether the book keeps that framing device explicit or lets the story simply stand on its own.

  • Story 3 — Rishi Vāmadeva (stilling speech), told in full:

    • Vāmadeva introduced as a unique kind of ascetic: a śmaśāna-sādhaka in Kāśī, who performed his sādhanā directly in the cremation ground
    • his defining quest: to transcend the fear of death, and the fear of speech itself — vāk
    • he invokes not the Rudras, not fire — but the essence of Bhairava specifically in this Ekavaktra form, precisely because this is the svarūpa that sits eyes-closed, in padmāsana, silent
    • what he actually sees isn’t quite named as Yajñeśvara — in the silence of that particular night, he perceives something closer to a still, awakened presence, not the fire-god register the name might suggest
    • the appearance itself: Ekavaktra appears in the tongue of a dying flame — a spark of energy activating within it — seated on a bed of ashes, with eyes that carry the “memory of time itself,” now active
    • Vāmadeva asks — silently, or as silence itself — “Guru, in which svarūpa have you shown yourself? Please tell me, so I may know which particular Devī or power I am meant to activate.”
    • Bhairava smiles and answers: “A single drop of yellow ash on my tongue is none other than —” [the specific syllable/name given here is unclear in the audio — flag for guru]
    • at that exact moment, Vāmadeva’s own speech stops, his mind dissolves, and time itself seems to pause — the teaching happens to him in real time, not just about him
    • this practice has two distinct purposes — worth being precise about which is which:
      • stilling the speech of an enemy — this is the purpose tied to Bagalāmukhī specifically, the silencing/stambhana power discussed under The associated Mahāvidyā
      • stilling one’s own incessant thoughts, one’s own tongue and mind — a separate, inward-facing purpose, not about anyone else at all: rishimonis would invoke Bhairava to still their own tongue and mind before entering the deepest sādhanā, so that speech stops, mind dissolves, and the mind itself can fully dissolve within that timeless, quiet space
      • the Vāmadeva story is specifically an account of the second kind — his own mind stopping, not an enemy’s
    • the inward version is described as producing a state resembling nirvikalpa samādhi — an inner quietude, thought having fully stopped
    • Vāmadeva sits 21 days in absolute samādhi in the cremation ground, and emerges holding the Ekavaktra mantra
    • named as one of the invokers connected to the Kaula Tantra
    • later becomes guru to several Nātha siddhas — his name is still recited within the Nātha tradition today

  • Story 4 — Gorakhnāth:

    • encountered this svarūpa while doing tapas in a salt cave near present-day Pakistan, close to Hinglaj Mātā’s site
    • received a stambhana-kriyā (breath-control technique) through a flame that emerged from his own navel
    • this is presented as the origin point for Kāya Yoga — later established Bhairava-pīṭhas for this specific svarūpa in Nepal, Assam, and Gujarat
    • explicitly not public teaching — held privately (gopya), transmitted only guru-to-disciple

  • Note: these four stories are not presented as a single continuous myth — they’re separate invocation accounts, each establishing a different facet (the name Yajñeśvara, the stambhana-mudrā, the speech-stilling practice, the breath technique/Kāya Yoga). Worth deciding in the writing whether to keep them as four distinct accounts (truer to the source) or thread them into one narrative arc.

  • Story 5 — Madanāsura, and the Bagalāmukhī–Bhairava union (told in full here; summarized again under The associated Mahāvidyā for reference):

    • Madanāsura is a demon who received a boon that no one could ever silence him — this is his defining trait, and the reason the rest of the story happens at all
    • he received that boon from [uncertain — the audio suggests “Hindata,” possibly Hinglaj Mātā herself; flag for guru]
    • the guru’s framing, stated directly and worth carrying into the book almost verbatim: any being of asuric or devatā nature always turns, in the end, to Śiva and Śakti — and Śakti has always held the power to grant any boon a sādhaka earns through genuine penance, regardless of whether that sādhaka is asuric or devic in nature. Madanāsura earned his boon the same way a devotee would.
    • Bagalāmukhī performs severe penance near [a specific Śakti Pīṭha, name uncertain] specifically to still Madanāsura’s speech — the only way to counter a boon that made him unsilenceable
    • the problem: her own tapas shook the sky, but her stambhana power alone was incomplete — paired with an as-yet-unsettled Śiva-aligned presence within her, she could not fully control the very śakti that had empowered Madanāsura in the first place
    • so she sits in penance specifically at Hinglaj Mātā’s pīṭha, and through that calls on Rudra
    • Bhairava appears — not raging, not with sound, but as “a yellow silence of time itself” — and the union that follows happens explicitly not physically, but within inner samādhi
    • she becomes the form of stilled action; he becomes the source of silence; together they manifest as Pītāmbara Vidyā
    • what the talk does not narrate: the actual confrontation or defeat of Madanāsura himself — the story as told ends at the union/vidyā being formed, not with an explicit resolution of what happens to him. Worth asking the guru directly whether that resolution exists in a version he knows, since as it stands the demon’s fate is implied rather than shown.

The associated Mahāvidyā

  • Bagalāmukhī — his consort/associate
  • Goddess of silence — also known for paralyzing evil acts: her core power is stambhana, absolute stillness
  • Her iconography: shown gripping/pulling the tongue of a rākṣasa or asura, in the act of paralyzing him — her power specifically sits in her eyes, not just the gesture with the tongue
  • Her temple in Kangra — well known; two dvārapālakas (gatekeepers) flank it, one as [uncertain — possibly Kāla Bhairava], the other as Hanumān
  • Called Stambhinī — “the one who stills” — anyone facing a plot against their life turns to her; described as the only Mother who can help through that specific crisis
  • Her etymology: Bagalāmukhī means “the one who grips the tongue of the ego”
  • Described as a paralyzing force, a fierce/heated gaze, singular in vision — contrasted directly against his single-faced stillness
  • Contemporary/real-world usage — worth including as its own point: she is heavily invoked for silencing enemies specifically. Politicians in particular are known to commission pūjās and prayogas through her to silence rivals, or to compel them to back down or act in a desired way. This isn’t presented as historical or mythic only — it’s a live, present-day practice.
  • Her origin story: performed severe penance near [a specific Śakti Pīṭha, name uncertain] to still the speech of the demon Madanāsura, who had received a boon that no one could ever silence him
    • the boon itself was granted to Madanāsura by [uncertain — the audio suggests “Hindata,” possibly Hinglaj Mātā herself]
    • her own tapas shook the sky, but her stambhana power was incomplete on its own — paired with an as-yet-unsettled Śiva-aligned presence in her, she wasn’t fully able to control the very śakti that had empowered Madanāsura
    • she then sat in penance specifically at Hinglaj Mātā’s pīṭha, and through that penance called on Rudra — this is where her stambhana power was bestowed by the silent Bhairava (this Ekavaktra form) directly
  • The union with Bhairava — explicitly not physical, psycho-spiritual: he appeared before her not raging, not with sound, but as a yellow silence of time itself; the union happened within inner samādhi, not physically
    • she became the form of stilled action; he became the source of silence
    • together, they manifested as Pītāmbara Vidyā
  • Pītāmbara Vidyā — carries a secret mantra tied directly to Yajñeśvara (his other name), capable of silencing action, karma, speech, an enemy, and even death itself
    • explicitly guarded knowledge — not published anywhere, transmitted only guru-to-disciple, only through inner vibration, only after the seeker completes several sādhanās within this vidyā first
  • A separate, rarer origin account: she is also said to have emerged from a golden lake — Haridrā Sarovara (turmeric lake) — through her own “roaring silence and forceful speech,” again in response to Madanāsura
  • What the union produced, specifically:
    • Mahā-Stambhana — the state through which she was able to take hold of Madanāsura’s asuric nature; described as the spiritual state where the sādhaka enters timelessness, the ego silences, speech becomes mantra, breath slows
    • the Pītāmbara-Stambhana Mahāmantra
    • what’s called “the secret breath” — rooted in Kashmir Shaivism’s dhāraṇās and Kāya Yoga
  • Together, described as the twin poles of inner tantra: his half is inner stillness; her half is outer silence
  • Combined power in sādhana: when her yantra is combined with his, it’s described as becoming an extraordinarily potent tool — usable only by advanced practitioners, for calming a restless mind, excess karma/inner fire, or pressing external enemies
  • Her overall register, elementally: complete, pure awareness; a power of silence specifically because she was able to still all asuric natures — framed as the feminine counterpart to his inner eye of stillness
  • She represents Śakti’s power to dissolve dual perception — opening the way toward realizing Spanda, the inner pulsation of consciousness

Introduction

  • Seventh of the Daśa Mahā Bhairava
  • Framed by the guru, at the start of the talk, as one of the least spoken-about of the ten
  • Directly paired with Mātaṅgī, ninth of the Daśa Mahāvidyā
  • Not a “couple” in the conventional sense — twin svarūpas occupying the same domain: inner speech, sound, what orthodoxy discards
  • Svarūpa, not avatāra — a distinction the guru is emphatic about, and worth establishing up front since it recurs across the whole book, not just this chapter
    • avatāra = completes a full human life cycle
    • svarūpa = arises already whole, for a purpose — he doesn’t develop into Mātaṅga Bhairava, he simply is it, from the moment he arises
    • relevant here specifically because Mātaṅga Bhairava is said to arise directly from Parama Śiva’s third eye, not from a womb — the distinction is what makes that origin make sense

Description

  • Described first and centrally as a Chāṇḍāla — an outcaste
  • Not a demon, not fearsome in the register of Kāla Bhairava — a wandering ascetic figure
  • Marked by what the caste order of the time held to be impure:
    • matted hair (jaṭā)
    • body covered in ash
    • dogs at his side
    • a skull-drum
    • appears in the cremation ground
  • Born not from a womb — arises directly from the Icchā Śakti of Parama Śiva, specifically from Śiva’s third eye
  • Resides in, and arises from, the Mahā-Śmaśāna — the great cremation ground
    • not just where he “appears” — this is framed as his origin point
    • the cremation ground represents the transmutation of death into liberation — the guru returns to this specifically to explain why the setting isn’t incidental: it’s the one place every person, regardless of caste, actually ends up

Historical veracity

Stories and mythological events

  • Why this svarūpa exists — the caste crisis
    • not framed as one myth among many — a direct response to a specific historical/spiritual crisis
    • a period when ritual arrogance around caste had reached its peak
    • people consumed by caste division: who could perform which rites, recite which mantras, which groups were told they could not follow certain practices
    • even the devatās, in his telling, struggled to understand what was happening
  • Temples became gatekept — this is where outcaste oppression rises
    • only certain castes considered fit to recite particular mantras, or to be heard at all
    • people pushed to the margins, denied participation in worship on the basis of birth rather than devotion
    • rituals performed correct in form but without bhava — mechanical, hollow, carried out by priests and kings who’d lost the thread
  • The response: Śiva takes outcaste form himself
    • not Bhairava rescuing outcastes from the outside — Paramaśiva becomes the Chāṇḍāla
    • guru’s direct point: Śiva’s grace does not belong to any one caste, any one set of people performing his worship
    • by taking the lowest, most excluded social form available, demonstrates — publicly, permanently, through his own myth — that access to the divine was never meant to be caste-gated
  • Why the cremation ground matters / why “outcaste” is the point, not incidental
    • looks to ordinary society like the place of the untouchable and discarded — people avoid it, believing negativity lingers there
    • but it’s also, in his framing, the one place every human being ends up, regardless of caste
    • the Chāṇḍāla form isn’t costume — it’s an argument, embodied, against the ritual hierarchy that made outcastes of some and gatekeepers of others
  • [Cross-reference: this is also where Uchchhiṣṭa — “the left-out” — shows up as a structural idea, not just a ritual category. Worth reconciling against Ch. 3’s guṇa material — see the flagged tension on whether Uchchhiṣṭa means “all three guṇas combined” or “the discarded remnant.”]
  • Small note, not a tangent: the talk also places Mātaṅga within the Aṣṭabhairava as one aspect of Ruru Bhairava — worth a single line acknowledging how Mātaṅga came about through that connection, but this book isn’t the place to open up Ruru Bhairava himself.

The associated Mahāvidyā

  • Mātaṅgī — ninth of the Daśa Mahāvidyā, and Mātaṅga Bhairava’s consort
  • Devatā of Vāk — speech — and specifically inner speech
  • Also devatā of music and nāda (sound) — “goddess of speech as well as sound”
  • Embodies the paradox of “impure purity” — sits above/beyond both orthodoxy and outcaste status, not claimed by either
  • Her origin — [IMPORTANT]: per Tantra/Āgama texts, most prominently connected to Mahā Pārvatī
    • when Devī Pārvatī, in her Uchchhiṣṭa state, created a powerful form capable of holding knowledge beyond logic and beyond convention — that form became Uchchhiṣṭa Mātaṅgī
    • she was born outside the boundaries of Vedic tradition — not a product of orthodox lineage
    • connected instead to the Mātaṅga / hunter lineage
    • this is specifically what makes her a representation of wild power — power drawn from those who lived wild, in the forest, outside caste and convention, as outcastes
    • she is a forest-dwelling goddess, and that wildness is not incidental to her — it’s the same quality that connects her directly to Mātaṅga’s own Chāṇḍāla origin
    • acted as the Uchchhiṣṭa — the remnant left behind — in orthodox rituals performed impurely, i.e. without bhāva
  • Described as a forest-dwelling tantric figure — a tantrasī who roams the forest, ruling over mantra-sound from the Vāma Mārga perspective
  • Connected to Mātaṅga through mystical melodies — ties to Nāda Ārādhana (worship through sound), part of activating the Śiva Sūtras, especially from the Vijñāna Bhairava Tantra perspective — teaches that liberation can arise through divine sound
  • Guru frames her as holding secret wisdom essential to understanding both Dakṣiṇa and Vāma Mārga
    • Dakṣiṇa = the Vedic path
    • Vāma Mārga = the occult, unseen energetic powers that can nonetheless be felt
  • Her domain is beyond social rules — described as a forest-dwelling tantric Sarasvatī, entirely outside social convention (distinct from the more orthodox Sarasvatī)
  • Why bhāva matters here: without it, the connection between devotee and Devī is incomplete; rituals performed mechanically, without bhāva, yield no result — ties back to the caste-crisis material in Stories and mythological events (empty ritual vs. felt devotion)
  • The consort pairing — why they’re paired at all, not just a myth detail:
    • both described as carrying the same pattern, occupying the same domain — this parallel patterning is the guru’s actual stated reason they’re consort, not incidental to it
    • Mātaṅgī — unorthodox, outside social propriety, forest-dwelling, born outside Vedic boundaries (see her origin above)
    • Mātaṅga Bhairava — the same shape, mirrored on the masculine side: Chāṇḍāla, outcaste, outside orthodoxy, born outside the womb/lineage system entirely
    • both are Uchchhiṣṭa — the “leftover” — each in their own register: for him, unmanifested speech and wisdom, the “leftover” offerings traditionally given to him; for her, the same wildness and destruction of orthodoxy
    • guru is careful to call this a svarūpa-level connection, not a “relationship” in the ordinary sense — he specifically corrects himself on this word
    • said to be born of Mātaṅgī’s own unstruck sound — Anāhata Nāda
    • together they’re described through Spanda — the living throb of consciousness, ever-vibrating, at a subtle, still level
  • Her own iconography — [confirming it’s covered]:
    • shown as emerald green — the color tied directly to her forest/wild association
    • holds a vīṇā — her core ritual implement, tying her to nāda/sound (the same vīṇā he also holds — the shared element between them)
    • a parrot is her identifying iconographic marker
    • called the “Chaṇḍī of the forest” — every cremation ground and forest carries mystical sounds, and those sounds are said to be governed by, and belong to, her specifically
    • overall persona: calm, enchanting — an embodiment of inner liberation, distinct in register from his fire
  • His role — keeper of secrets, silent guardian, despite the element of fire [make sure this lands clearly]:
    • almost every other svarūpa of Bhairava is governed by, and expressed through, fire — wrath, burning, consuming
    • Mātaṅga Bhairava is the exception: here he is silent
    • specifically described as guardian of secret yogas, tantric secrets — the keeper, not the revealer
    • the fire element is still his — it’s how he’s governed elementally — but it shows up as containment and silence rather than open display, which is part of what distinguishes him from the other nine
    • worth stating plainly in the writing: this quietness is not the absence of his fire, it’s what fire looks like when its job is guarding a secret rather than burning something away
  • Elemental / iconographic contrast between them:
    • Mātaṅgī — calm, enchanting, embodiment of inner liberation (see her own iconography above)
    • Mātaṅga Bhairava — by contrast, though he carries fire as his element, he is silent, ash-covered, radiant/burning; carries triśūla, ḍamaru, and also a vīṇā
  • Where each one “belongs”: Mātaṅgī is tied to the forest broadly; Mātaṅga Bhairava specifically to the Mahā-Śmaśāna (great cremation ground) — the guru’s shorthand distinction between them despite the shared domain
  • The physical pairing image from the talk: he sits with her within a circle of flames, burning away impurities; she holds the vīṇā representing tantric nāda; he also holds a vīṇā, resonating with śabda brahma, the divine sound — because he himself arose from nāda when Śiva brought him forth from the third eye
  • She is described sitting at his left side, whispering mantras into the ear, awakening the devotee to Śiva
  • Their shared mastery — mantra, music, communication:
    • attaining mastery over mantra, music, and communication is named directly as the essence of both — “the essence of Bhairava, and the essence of Mātaṅga”
    • this is given as the reason not to fix on a single svarūpa: when Bhairava’s ārādhana is performed, all his svarūpas activate together, Mātaṅga’s attributes included — mystical music, mystical mantra, mystical communication all connect back to him
    • once activated, social impurity and purity — and duality itself — are said to be crushed entirely, swept away, not just balanced or softened

8. Pañcavaktra Bhairava

Introduction

including temples and sites where this svarūpa is encountered

The five-faced lord

Pañcavaktra — pañca-vaktra, the five faces. In Shaivism the five faces represent him as Parama Śiva himself, and he calls this svarūpa Sadāśiva guṇa.

Deva of mokṣa

As the five-faced lord he is the deva of mokṣa — the lord of liberation for all existence and all creation in this cosmos.

This is the claim the whole chapter has to earn, and it is not decorative. Every other Bhairava in the ten does something to you — removes, burns, clears. This one is the exit itself.

Five mirrors of the self

The frame he gives before anything else: he is seen as five mirrors of the self, each a reflection of the one flame of supreme Śiva.

Not five deities. Not five moods. Five reflections of a single flame — which is why the chapter can end where it does, with his svarūpa may be five, but his guṇa is one.

His place in the Vedas

Not under the name Bhairava — as Pañcavaktra he connects directly to the Vedas. His essence there is encrypted in the codes.

Through that lens he is the five-faced intelligence of ṛta — of divine rhythm, the embodiment of cosmic law. The four Vedas evolved out of Brahmā; ṛta is the cosmic order Śiva established in this svarūpa.

And how he is met there is different. In the Vedic stream he is less visualised and more realised — through order, vibration, and tapas. The stream comes bound with rules; you raise your vibration and you do your tapas. Realisation is available. Vision is not the method.

The other Vedic fivefolds — pañcāgni (five fires), pañcabhūta, pañcadiśā (five directions) — all connect finally to Pañcavaktra. And the point the Vedic ritual is actually making: they are not outside. They are inside.

Description

The five faces

Each face carries a function, an element, and — in the Kaula reading — a Śakti.

1. Sadyojāta — creation

The creator face. In Kaula he is himself called Kāmeśvara, paired with Kāmeśvarī — the deva of desire, of awakening consciousness. And when Śakti is seated in the lap of Śiva, that is Sadyojāta. (Element unclear in the talk — the audio conflicts with Aghora being given fire moments later. Conventionally earth. [ASK GURU])

2. Vāmadeva — preservation

Soma. Water. His Śakti is Bhagamālinī, or Bhairavī herself. What this union brings the sādhaka: bliss, and the nurturing of the soul.

3. Aghora — destruction

The Rudra portion. Fire — and he is careful that this is a different fire from agni elsewhere. His Śakti is Vajravāsinī / Vajreśvarī, and what happens here is the transformation of the ego. Anyone in the Aghora mārga who activates this third face is dissolving their ego.

4. Tatpuruṣa — concealment

The masculine face. Vāyu. Air. His Śakti is Mahāvajreśvarī, and the formula given is breath becomes mantra — Namaḥ Śivāya. This is the Śiva-Śakti combination proper.

5. Īśāna — revelation

Ākāśa. Ether. What dissolves here is the illusion of “me, myself.”

The pañcākṣara — a mirror of the five elements

Namaḥ Śivāya is the pañcākṣara, the five-syllabled mantra.

And here is what he calls the secret: the pañcākṣara is a powerful mirror of the five elements of energy.

It is stated in the Liṅga Purāṇa that whoever recites Om Namaḥ Śivāya takes control of the five elements — but not from outside. The five elements must first become active in oneself, so that he can be understood, seen, and experienced through all the senses. The five senses are what the five energies are.

Which means: knowingly or unknowingly, anyone performing the pañcākṣara is activating Pañcavaktra Bhairava. Anyone who has ever said Om Namaḥ Śivāya has already been in this chapter’s territory without knowing it. That is the hook.

Syllable by syllable

NA — earth. The power of action, kriyā śakti. Grounding — it takes us out of the physical world and begins the journey on the spiritual plane.

MA — water. The power of desire, icchā. 75% of the human body is water; 75% of bhūloka is water. Ma is emotion, and the soul’s longing to reunite with its source — going back to our roots.

ŚI — fire. The power of knowledge, jñāna śakti. The inner flame. Every one of us carries it, and it burns ignorance and awakens wisdom.

VĀ — air. The power of movement — and movement is essential to every sādhaka. This is effort, guiding the sādhaka consciously along the path.

YA — ether, space. The power of grace, anugraha śakti. Here duality — me, mine — dissolves, and the soul merges into Śiva, into infinite consciousness.

Namaḥ Śivāya is not just a word. These five syllables together are the complete embodiment of Pañcavaktra Śiva — five faces, five elements, five-folded energies. Experienced, they carry us from matter to spirit, and the duality merges.

The five faces as the five stages of liberation

This is the payoff of the chapter, and he gives it near the end almost in passing: the five faces are also the five stages by which a person is actually liberated.

1. Śravaṇa — hearing. Listening about him, more and more. The connection with the inner guru activates by itself — you do not have to do anything else at this stage but listen.

2. Manana — reflection. Reflecting on him constantly. Thinking of him. Offering every one of your actions at his lotus feet.

3. Nididhyāsana — meditation, observation, absorption. Even seated in meditation, a dormant mind stays active. Reciting him, thinking of him, connecting with him inside that dormant stage — that too is nididhyāsana.

4. Anubhava — direct experience. After 144 days. He states it plainly: complete your three maṇḍalas, and in prathamācaraṇa you will experience Bhairava. That direct experience enters your life.

5. Turya — the highest state. The transcendence of all states. Activated through certain mudrās and kriyās — khecarī mudrā is one route. Turya is the final stage, where you become the guru yourself.

Why this matters structurally. The chapter opened by calling him the deva of mokṣa. This is the mechanism of that claim. The five faces are not a description of him — they are the staircase.

The five acts — pañcakṛtya

Sadāśiva is the five acts, and these five are what Pañcavaktra Bhairava refers to:

  1. Sṛṣṭi — creation
  2. Sthiti — preservation
  3. Saṃhāra — destruction
  4. Tirobhāva — concealment
  5. Anugraha — grace

And he performs them in an ordinary life, not only in cosmology. This is the passage that makes the doctrine land — consider ourselves, as humans:

  • We do sṛṣṭi — we create
  • We do sthiti — every minute we are maintaining something: the home, the family, the office
  • We do saṃhāra — when someone troubles us we destroy; if not externally then internally, we simply stop caring for them
  • We do tirobhāva — concealment; every one of us does it
  • And we all wait for anugraha — grace. Having that person in my life is a grace.

So the five acts are not separate from Pañcavaktra at all. You have been performing them all day.

Established here, before the streams, because this is who he is: Sadāśiva is the five acts. Every stream below is a different way of approaching the same five.

A note on the streams

Each of the five ācāras approaches him differently. What follows is a brief description of each as it bears on this svarūpa — the Vedic reading is in the Introduction above; Kaula, Dakṣiṇa and Vāma follow. [EXPAND] — Siddhānta ācāra is not treated in this talk and needs its own brief description.

The Kaula stream

Kaula tantra mārga: a union between your living existence and the flame — Parama Śiva. There is a mantra holding that the five-faced one is the supreme Śiva guṇa abiding in the heart of the Kaulas, and that whoever knows, touches, and experiences these five elements is part of Śiva.

The cakra

Here ārādhana is performed through the cakra form — the maṇḍala, and the maṇḍala is a living energy, not a diagram.

Two everyday proofs he offers:

  • Across much of South India it is still the practice to draw the rangoli before the house each morning. That is a cakra — a sacred maṇḍala which is a living power.
  • In the temple, when the deity is activated, they draw a star, and at each junction take a little jala and draw. This is activating the prāṇa śakti in that maṇḍala — the caturasra maṇḍala. It is the activation of the energies of the five elements.

Nyāsa — the five faces are ours

In Kaula, the ārādhana of Pañcavaktra is performed through nyāsa — because nyāsa is the mapping of the five faces onto one’s own body.

We carry the five faces.

This is the hinge of the whole chapter. The five faces are not out there being described. They are being located — on the practitioner. Everything the chapter says about creation, preservation, destruction, concealment and revelation is a statement about what is already in the body performing the nyāsa.

Śakti pūjā — and why one cannot be worshipped without the other

The Śakti pūjā here is connected to Bālā Tripurasundarī. And the image: when Śakti is seated in the lap of Śiva, that is Sadyojāta — the first face.

And then the rule. In Kaula tantra, if you perform the ārādhana of Śiva alone, it is incomplete. Unless you merge Śiva with Śakti, liberation — the dissolution of illusion — does not come into existence at all.

This is the same claim as Part One §10. Cross-reference rather than restate.

Bhāva samādhi

The five faces dissolving into the inner self. Whatever five elements, whatever five guṇas we carry as individuals — dissolving all of them into the inner self is bhāva samādhi.

And bhāvana is what makes this possible, because bhāvana is what produces real surrender: you may think it is me, myself, doing this. It is he himself doing everything in you.

A living entity

In the Kaula mārga, Pañcavaktra is not merely worship. He is a living entity.

Here he is connected as Sadāśiva guṇa tattva. And the image given for what that means in practice: he is the fire within the cremation of the ego. Whatever we carry as ego is itself the cremation ground — and he is the only one who can remain alive in that ground, and connect us from there to the mokṣa mārga.

The Dakṣiṇa stream — the right-hand path

Here Pañcavaktra is worshipped as Sadāśiva — the regulating, meditating Lord Śiva.

Method

Ārādhana here is in the simplest form — not elaborate ritual. Discipline and devotion are essential. Self-purification is essential. But ritual offering is held highest, and mokṣa comes through order and through surrender.

Japa is connected to all five faces — and across vāmācāra, kaulācāra and dakṣiṇācāra alike, japa is the highest form of reaching Śiva guṇa, of reaching Bhairava.

What dakṣiṇācāra is finally about: surrender.

The Vāma stream — and a correction

“Left-hand path” is the label, and he corrects it.

What vāmācāra actually is: liberation through shock and through flame.

And it is not for everyone. He says so directly, without softening it.

What happens in it

Here Śiva as Pañcavaktra is in ugra svarūpa — extremely fierce. But the fierceness has a function: he is freeing us from the māyās that surround us and dilute us in this existence. Śiva in the fierce form is making the point this is not the way for you.

Each of the five faces burns away a layer of ego.

And what goes is more than ego:

  • He removes the ego
  • He removes the illusion
  • He removes social conditioning — good, bad, ugly; this is what should be done; this is first, then second, then third

In vāmācāra he removes everything.

There is no order in it

This is the structural difference from every other stream. There is no sequence here. No first stage, then second, then third. What there is instead is synchronisation between the seeker and the giver — between the sādhaka and the creator.

The fivefold flames

Connected to Bālā Tripurasundarī, he awakens the fivefold flames within oneself.

Everyone wants that. Almost nobody gets it, and the reasons are ordinary rather than tragic — no time, other priorities, I have already read this. Reading is a concept. (Part One §12.)

The inner guru

This is the centre of the chapter and the reason it is not simply a description of a deity.

Pañcavaktra is not something outside. The guru is not somewhere outside. The guru is within us.

You may think the guru is someone sitting and speaking about a deity — that he is the guru. What is actually happening: that Śiva, that Bhairava, is speaking to the Bhairava within you. When that Bhairava is activated, that is the inner guru. And once it is activated, you understand what this self is capable of.

Where this is taught, and where it is not. He notes that this activation is not spoken of in Dakṣiṇa. It is told in Vāmācāra and Kaulācāra. Worth flagging: the streams do not merely differ in method, they differ in what they will tell you about yourself.

And the responsibility clause, which he does not soften: Pañcavaktra is the fivefold rhythm of our own awakening self, sitting in dormant stages. Activating it is your own responsibility. No one is going to do it on your behalf. Not your wife. Not your husband.

The stages of that activation

The five faces are also the five stages by which the inner guru comes awake — śravaṇa, manana, nididhyāsana, anubhava, turya. Set out in full under Description above; this is where they belong doctrinally, so cross-reference rather than repeat.

Two things worth restating here:

  • Anubhava — direct experience — is placed at 144 days. Complete the three maṇḍalas and in prathamācaraṇa you will experience Bhairava.
  • Turya is where you become the guru yourself. Which closes the loop the section opened: the inner guru is not a metaphor for having a good teacher. It is the endpoint.

The problem of duality

The whole thing turns on one obstacle.

Unless the five guṇas of Śiva connect with the five tattvas of Śakti, the inner guru cannot be activated.

Pañcavaktra is Śiva who holds the secrets of kaula bhoga — the divine enjoyment. But that connection has to be established simultaneously burning away the duality — I am separate, you are separate. Until that burns, he is never seen as deva svarūpa at all.

Kaulika bhoga: kaulika, divine; bhoga, enjoyment. And it happens only when duality is dissolved.

The same obstacle appears in a second form: the sādhaka who worships in one mārga and declares another wrong. Do you think Śiva split himself into five faces so that one would be good and another wrong? That is duality wearing the clothes of piety.

Closing — he is not an external deity

The line the chapter should end on:

The supreme is not distant. He is formless. Bhairava is alive. He is intimate.

He is not sitting in some cosmos or some galaxy. He is sitting here — in oneself, in the cosmos of yourself. All five elements you carry. All five guṇas you carry. All the pañcabhūtas you carry.

Awakening the self is awakening the Bhairava in yourself.

Kāśī — the site

He is the Kotwal of Kāśī — the sheriff, the chief of police, the protector of the city. Not a resident deity but an office-holder.

And the office is enforced literally: no one enters or resides in Kāśī without his permission. Devotees seek his blessing before beginning anything else there — some sources call it the Kashi visa, and the convention is that you go to him before Kāśī Viśvanātha.

The story that puts him there is the story of this chapter. Carrying the Brahma kapāla, he wanders for redemption. At Kāśī the skull falls — the site is Kapāla Mocana Tīrtha — and the sin is destroyed. Śiva then appoints him permanent guardian of the city’s boundaries. The twelve-year parikrama does not end anywhere; it ends here, and the ending is what installs him.

Names given at that moment (attributed to the Skanda Purāṇa / Kāśī Khaṇḍa — to verify): - Kālarāja — because even Kāla fears him - Amardaka — because his anger suppresses the wicked-minded - Pāpabhakṣaṇa — devourer of sins, because he consumes his devotees’ sins in a moment

The fingernail

He severs Brahmā’s fifth head with the tip of his fingernail. Not a weapon. Not the trident, not the sword. The smallest edge he has.

What that establishes: the removal costs him nothing. There is no struggle in it, no contest between Bhairava and the creator. The ego that had convinced itself it was supreme is taken off with the least thing on his body. The disproportion is the teaching.

And what he removes is not Brahmā. It is the fifth head — the specific part that had begun claiming supremacy, and the part that spoke the lie. The rest of the creator remains, and continues to create. This is a surgical act, not an annihilating one.

What that same act looks like in a life

The talk is explicit that this is not a one-time mythological event. It is what happens when the ārādhana is taken up: the unnecessary things in one’s life will be chopped off. Whenever the truth is not being told, he removes it.

How it actually shows up:

  • People come and go. Relationships that were load-bearing turn out not to be. Some end in blow-ups; some simply thin out and stop.
  • Some of it will look like loss of abundance. Money, position, arrangements that felt secure.
  • Some of it goes without event. People fall away and there is no scene, no reason given, no closure offered.
  • New people arrive. The clearing is not only subtraction.
  • The problems appear to multiply, not resolve. The talk says this outright — people write in saying their difficulties have increased tenfold, twentyfold, a hundredfold. The answer given is: yes. It will happen. Whatever prārabdha is carried has to come up to be cleared.

The frame the tradition puts on it: the diamond. In the raw state, what is stuck to it has to be struck off, cut, and shaped before anything glitters. The stone is not being damaged. It is being finished.

None of it is punishment, and none of it is arbitrary. It is all in service of your own liberation — an uninterrupted line toward him, with whatever was interrupting it taken out of the way.

And the hard clause, which the talk does not soften: sādhana is not a love affair, and it is not easy-go. You are here to lose. If you are not ready to lose, you are not yet entitled to the paddhati.

Story 1 — The pillar of light, the fifth head, and the twelve years

The fifth head. Brahmā creates the four Vedas, and then a fifth. Holding the fifth head, he begins to think himself equal to everyone — equal to Parama Śiva himself.

The cosmos trembles. Not because of an external threat, but because the creators themselves have begun arguing on an egoistic level — Brahmā and Viṣṇu, over which of them is the more prominent, the more powerful. And the system they are quarrelling over was created by the Devī herself.

The pillar. Neither yields. At Tiruvannamalai, Śiva takes the form of a stambha — an infinity of light, Sadāśiva manifesting as a beam in infinite expansion. This is Aruṇācaleśvara. The form is not portraying himself; it is portraying the essence of the creation of the cosmos.

(Site note: this is why the parikrama of the hill is performed. Tiruvannamalai is held as the prathama svarūpa of Śiva, witnessed by the entire cosmos, and is called Dakṣiṇa Kāśī — the Kāśī of the south. The siddhars are there; the seventy-two siddhas took form there; Ramaṇa Maharṣi among them.)

The wager. Whoever finds the end of the pillar will be declared supreme in the cosmos of creation.

  • Brahmā takes the form of a swan and goes upward along the ākāśa mārga, seeking the top.
  • Viṣṇu takes the form of the boar, Varāha, and goes downward toward Pātālaloka, seeking the base.

Neither finds it.

The lie. Brahmā enlists the Ketakī flower, who takes his side and testifies falsely before Śiva that she has seen the tip of the stambha. Śiva lays a curse upon her.

The emergence. But the response to Brahmā is of a different order. The third eye opens, and from the ājñā cakra Kāla Bhairava takes svarūpa — not an avatāra, a svarūpa. He comes to teach the creator that his creation is not appropriate: ego cannot be part of creation. Ego cannot drive the creator’s creation.

The severing. He removes the fifth head — the head that claimed supremacy, and the head that spoke the lie — with the tip of his fingernail.

Brahmahatyā. The skull adheres to his hand. He now bears the sin of it, and he carries the Brahma kapāla — which is none other than the fifth Veda, the thing the creator himself was not able to hold. Brahmā may have created that information; he could not carry it.

The twelve years. He is set to take the parikrama of the entire Kāśī kṣetra — of the whole sṛṣṭi, all the lokas — carrying the skull. Twelve years on his time scale. At the end of it, at Kāśī, the skull falls.

Story 2 — Vaikuṇṭha, and the teaching given to Viṣṇu

The half of the story most people do not know. The talk says so outright: we know the Brahmā side; what happened with Viṣṇu is the part that goes untold.

The invitation. After the beheading, Viṣṇu bows before Bhairava and asks him to come to Vaikuṇṭha. Bhairava does not come immediately — see He comes in his own time below.

The arrival. When he does come, Jaya sees him at the gates and sends word that something furious is standing outside — we feel this is a vetāla; we feel he is not part of the deva guṇa.

Viṣṇu runs to the door. He is at rest, with Lakṣmī in his presence. Without any delay — exactly as in the Kṛṣṇa and Sudāmā episode — he runs to receive him himself. Because this is none other than Śiva Paramātmā at his doorstep, carrying the kapāla, holding the khaḍga, and he has come not to punish anyone but to give darśana to the creator.

Why this matters structurally. The Daśāvatāra of Viṣṇu is said to be supported by the Daśa Mahā Bhairavas. Whenever an avatāra came forth in its furious nature — Narasiṃha, Vāmana, and Kalki still to come — the furious nature of Viṣṇu is Bhairava.

The teaching. The devatās ask why he came at all. And Bhairava gives his gurutva teaching to Viṣṇu himself: this is not about violation. It is about liberation.

And then the turn. Brahmā was not the only one. Viṣṇu also carried pride — he had an equal share in that same crime of claiming supremacy before the five-headed Śiva. His ego is not removed by a fingernail. It is dissolved by being made to articulate what karma actually is. What follows is the teaching Viṣṇu then gave to all of humankind.

Guru teachings

The removal of falsehood

Dharma carries truth and righteousness in it. Bhairava is what enforces that — the dispenser of karma, and the remover of falsehood.

The talk states the principle flatly: whenever the truth is not being told, he will remove it. Brahmā’s fifth head was not speaking the truth. That is what came off.

And the framing he gives for the whole episode: this is not about violation. It is about liberation.

Honouring Bhairava is honouring the destruction of falsehood. Once he enters a life, the falsehood goes — and specifically the false of yourself. Whatever is called me, myself, mine will not be there at all.

The illusions that go first are your own. Not other people’s lies about you. Yours about yourself. Brahmā’s falsehood was not primarily the flower’s testimony; it was the prior conviction that he was supreme. The testimony was only the thing that made it audible.

Destroyer of ego. That is the first act, before anything else: he came into existence and his first task was chopping off the ego of the creator. Ego cannot be part of creation. Ego cannot drive the creator’s creation.

Be conscious — the teaching on awareness

The taxonomy is in the karma subchapter. This is the instruction that comes out of it.

The three things to watch: be conscious of your actions. Be conscious of your thoughts. Be conscious of your emotions. He says this is what the guru bhāva, the guru tattva, the gurujana will always tell you — not as encouragement, but as the working method.

And the result he promises for it is oddly specific: attend to those three, and you stop asking what you did and what result is coming. The question why me, why has this happened only to me, why not to someone else stops arising — not because it gets answered, but because you can already see the machinery.

Śukla karma — the white. Born of sāttvika. What makes an act śukla is not that it is pleasant or virtuous, but that you know you are doing it. I am eating consciously. I am picking this up deliberately. I know what is to be done. Not “it happened accidentally.” Awareness is the whole definition.

Kṛṣṇa karma — the black. Arises where tāmasika and rājasika combine — inertia joined to agitation. It covers selfishness, violence, ignorance.

And the clarification to carry: Kṛṣṇa here is not the name of the god. Kṛṣṇa is a guṇa. Kṛṣṇa is a consciousness — the meeting point of those two. Hence Kālī belongs here, and hence one of her names is Kṛṣṇa Kālī.

What the pair actually asks of you. Notice that the axis is not good and evil. It is conscious and unconscious. Kṛṣṇa karma is what gets made when inertia and agitation act through you and you are not present for it. Śukla is what gets made when you are.

Which means awareness is not a moral achievement here. It is the single variable. Every human carries all three shades, and the same act can fall either way depending only on whether anyone was home when it happened.

And the honest part: most action is neither. It is śukla-kṛṣṇa — mixed, partly good, partly bad, which he names as the ordinary human condition without disapproval. The calendar itself alternates, fifteen days and fifteen days, permanently.

Practical form: the 48-day sādhana spans both pakṣas deliberately, so that both the white and the black are covered inside one month.

Sādhana is not a love affair

He is blunt about this, and it should be quoted rather than paraphrased into something gentler.

It is not easy-go. It is not a love affair. If you want your life to become beautiful through the sādhana paddhati, understand what the paddhati actually is: the purification of yourself.

Which means it will get worse before it gets better. People write to him and text him constantly to say their problems have increased — tenfold, twentyfold, fiftyfold, a hundredfold — since taking up the ārādhana. His answer is not reassurance. It is: yes. It will happen.

The reason he gives is precise, and worth keeping: whatever prārabdha karma is being carried has to surface in order to be cleared. The turmoil is not the practice damaging you. It is the balance sheet coming up to be settled. That distinction matters — it is the difference between “this is harming me” and “this is what clearing looks like.”

The diamond. In the raw state, the unnecessary material fused to the stone has to be struck, chipped, cut away, and the stone shaped, before anything glitters. The diamond is not being destroyed in that process. It is being finished. This is what he is doing in a life.

It does lighten. Thoughts settle, situations settle. But the front end is turmoil, and anyone told otherwise has been sold something else.

Sādhana is about losing

The second half of the same teaching, and the one people most resist.

It is not about acquiring. Not knowledge, not siddhis, not outcomes. People arrive at the paddhati assuming they are there to gain, and he corrects it directly: you are here to lose everything.

Sādhana is all about losing. If you are ready to lose — only then are you entitled to come to the sādhana paddhati.

And the thing to be lost is the self. This is the lesson the creators themselves were given: unless the self is lost, liberation does not occur at all. Brahmā lost the head that claimed supremacy. Viṣṇu lost the pride he had carried into the same quarrel. Neither was destroyed; both were relieved of the part that had begun to claim.

Which is why the counting does not help. Someone who reports five years, six hundred days, ten maṇḍalas, and a list of unresolved problems has been keeping a checklist rather than doing a sādhana. In sādhana you are meant to forget yourself — including how many maṇḍalas you have done.

He comes in his own time

Viṣṇu invites him to Vaikuṇṭha. Bhairava does not go. He answers: when I do my parikrama, when I do my tīrtha yātrā, I will come one day to Vaikuṇṭha, and I will give my darśana there.

He arrives eventually — and when he does, he is received at the door with the utmost reverence. But the invitation and the arrival are not the same moment, and the gap between them belongs to him, not to the one who invited him.

The talk widens this immediately: even Viṣṇu, in taking the daśāvatāras, waited for his essential time before appearing, so that the svarūpa would be received as it should be.

What this means for the sādhaka. He does the same with us. He makes the sādhaka wait for the right time — for the thing to happen, for what is needed to arrive. Delay is not absence, and it is not refusal. Elsewhere the talk puts it plainly: you may get it, or it may be delayed, depending on your karma-phala.

And the harder clause: he may not grant it at all. That possibility is not a failure of the sādhana. It sits alongside the instruction not to enter into a transaction with him in the first place — and alongside the saṅkalpa he actually recommends: what is not important in my life, remove it; what is important, give me that. On those terms, an ungranted wish is not a denial. It is an answer.

Story 1 — The marriage of Kāmeśvara and Lalitā Tripurasundarī

Narrated by Śrī Hayagrīva — Viṣṇu in the horse-faced form, the embodiment of knowledge, guru even to Sarasvatī.

The problem. Mahā Tripurasundarī manifests, and she is so beautiful that not one devatā in the assembly can identify who could possibly take her as consort. The measure given: put together crores of Ratis, and all of it amounts to one percent of a single blink of hers. Day after day the assembly is stuck on the same question — who could receive her?

The obvious answer nobody is comfortable with. The devatās keep arriving at Śiva. But Śiva is not socially acceptable.

Brahmā’s doubt — the detail that matters. He asks whether Lalitā would even accept Śiva. And the objection is specific and physical: - He is bhasma-dhara — smeared head to foot in cremation ash - He is a śmaśānavāsī — he lives in the burning ground - His hair is undone, matted in dreadlocks - He carries skulls - At his own earlier wedding he arrived surrounded by his gaṇas — spirits, ghosts, vetālas — the whole gaṇaloka trailing behind him - He rides a bull, arriving like a raw man, like an aghorī

Brahmā’s question is not malice. It is a genuine mismatch: is he even capable of holding her? Would this radiant goddess be pleased by that?

The hinge. Brahmā’s doubt is itself the cause. The svarūpa does not pre-exist the question — the question produces it. Brahmā’s thought was the root of the Kāmeśvara svarūpa.

The transformation. In that moment Śiva appears in the assembly: - Dazzling as ten thousand suns together — and not the sun we casually mean; the whole cosmos is governed by one such sun - No longer ash-smeared; radiant as fire - As though ten million suns, or trillions of Kāmadevas, merged into one form - Heavenly garlands the devatās had never seen - A divine fragrance that bewildered them — they could not find its source - A crown of jewels never before seen or imagined - The embodiment of irresistible charm

Even Hayagrīva, narrating, says he is short of words and can convey perhaps one percent of it.

The naming. Seeing this, Brahmā proclaims the word: Kāmeśvara. He is the first ever to be called by that name.

The meeting of eyes. The devatās escort him before her. He comes as Nīla Śiva, beautiful as the moon’s disc, an ocean of thousands of moons. Lalitā beholds him and takes his hand. Their eyes meet — and she feels Kāmadeva himself regaining form, because Śiva had burnt Kāmadeva to ash with the third eye. She recognises that he has returned as ten million Kāmadevas in one form. Her heart recognises him as her counterpart.

Her condition. Before she will accept him, she states it: I am forever independent. My play and my actions are my own, and my husband’s conduct must harmonise with me. Her knowledge, her actions, her speech — all of it her own.

The giving of her hand. Brahmā turns to Viṣṇu: you are her brother; per dharma you must offer her hand to Kāmeśvara Śiva.

The promise. Śiva answers her condition directly — you are not merely feminine energy to me, you are half of my life. This is the Ardhanārīśvara promise: you take whatever decisions you wish, you are entitled to decide, you are independent.

The union. They are married. When Kāmeśvara merges with Lalitā they become one, and that unified consciousness is Kāmeśvarī. From that day they are never spoken of as two svarūpas. Love and power joined, to balance the cosmos.

Source. Lalitopākhyāna; Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa, ch. 14 (possibly 15) — a set of roughly 117 ślokas.

Story 2 — Ṛṣi Vātsyāyana and the first Śrī Cakra pūjā

Source: the Bhairava Tantra material, with the darśana verses attributed to the Rudrayāmala Tantra.

Who he was. Vātsyāyana, son of Kaśyapa, living in deep forest at the foothills of the Vindhya hills. Well-versed in Vedas and Āgamas; among the most potent sages of his time. Through Kaśyapa he stands in the lineage that fathered the daitya gaṇa — the line running through Diti, and through Jaya and Vijaya in their rākṣasa births, which is the Narasiṃha story.

His practice. He was performing a great Aghora sādhana. Hundreds of years of penance — and ṛṣi years are not human years; some are said to remain in bodily form to this day.

His problem — and it is not a devotional problem. He had already attained darśana of both. He had understood Śiva tattva and Bhairava tattva. But only separately: - Śiva he perceived as vast and formless - Śakti he perceived as compassion and luminosity - He could not unite them in his heart

His questions were: What is Śakti? What is Kāmeśvarī? How does the merging of Devī and Śiva actually occur? And the one that matters most — how can a human being experience Śiva-guṇa and Devī-guṇa at one and the same time? We always see separation: masculine, feminine.

This was his anguish. Hundreds of years of penance had produced perception but not integration.

The scriptural statement that frames it. Śiva without Śakti is a corpse — śava. Śakti without Śiva is movement without a base.

The prescription. Kaśyapa conveys this to him, and Vātsyāyana resolves on a long tapas. His father instructs him to take up the mantra in which Devī and Śiva are worshipped together in the liṅga svarūpa, so that the complete formation of the svarūpa can be seen. Vātsyāyana follows the sādhana paddhati exactly as prescribed, step by step.

The first vision — and his refusal of it. In inner vision, out of a radiant emerging light, Pañcavaktra Bhairava appears — five-faced, holding trident, ḍamaru, rosary, and skull. The flame and fire of the form spark up and the ṛṣi trembles. He says plainly: Bhagavān, I cannot bear you in this five-headed form. Can I see you in a form through which I can understand you better?

The second vision. Kāmeśvarī comes — the merged consciousness of Kāmeśvara and Mahā Tripurasundarī — seated upon a throne. Her smile soft as moonlight, described as millions of moons radiating. Her eyes at once fiery and compassionate.

He is said to be the first ṛṣi ever to see the svarūpa of Bhairava Kāmeśvara — the sovereign of desires, the Bhagavān of the entire cosmos of union. Not worldly union, not one galaxy: the whole cosmos.

The verse: Kāmeśvara — and beside me is Kāmeśvarī.

He asks again. He asks to see them in their two separate forms, and Kāmeśvara and Kāmeśvarī come together upon the throne and grant it.

The teaching he receives on desire. His desire was not for bondage. To realise, you do not deny desire — by transmuting desire into divine will, one enters divine consciousness. The divine is not bondage. It is the fire that leads to freedom.

Kāmeśvarī’s instruction on method. Worship is not through the outward forms of the world. Fix on one particular form and you forget the essence. Hence, esoterically, the ārādhana is performed not with a mūrti and not with a form, but with the yantra. Hence also the pañcamakāra, in its three phases — mostly sāttvika, with tāmasika and rājasika only on certain days of the year.

The Śrī Cakra. Vātsyāyana becomes the first Maharṣi to perform Śrī Cakra sādhana. He is not its founder — he downloads it from consciousness, from the universe, and writes out the maṇḍala. (The Devī Purāṇa puts it differently: Devī herself took the form of the yantra.) He takes the union of Kāmeśvara and Lalitā Tripurasundarī as the Śrī Cakra itself.

He invokes: - Kāmeśvara at the bindu — the central dot - Kāmeśvarī Tripurasundarī at the trikoṇa — the central triangle

with mantras from the Rudrayāmala Tantra and the hymns of the Śrī Bālā Tripurasundarī Khaḍgamālā.

Twelve years. He performs the Śrī Cakra pūjā for twelve years. The nāga devatās support the process; devatās of all fourteen lokas participate in activating and energising the yantra. Offerings of puṣpam, honey, ghee, milk, curd, with the Pañcavaktra Bhairava mantra.

The night it opens. Reciting in moonlight, divine energy spouts from the cakra. He meditates on the union of Kāmeśvara and Kāmeśvarī. Bliss energy activates and burns away the impurities in his own creation.

Navamī. On the ninth day of Navarātri the final offering is made — and the yantra blazes into cosmic light. He trembles. The divine couple emerges in splendour out of the Śrī Cakra. A hand is placed upon his head.

What changed. His consciousness expanded. He no longer saw himself as separate. He became one with Śiva, one with Kāmeśvarī, one with Tripurasundarī — and beheld the universe as a play of union. Every sound, every mantra, every form of yantra, every devatā, every creation: nothing but the play of the union.

The definition he is given. Kāmeśvarī smiles and says that from this day Vātsyāyana is the first person to know Bhairava consciousness — and that Bhairava consciousness is not any one form of Bhairava. Not Kāla Bhairava, not Baṭuka, not Kapālī. It is the union itself: every breath of the mantra, every thought of offering, every union of liberation.

What he composed after. A Bālā Tripurasundarī stotra. And his teaching: true worship is not escaping desire. To escape is only to postpone. Transform it into divine love and bliss.

The proof by contradiction — the monk and the white elephant. A wealthy woman comes to a Buddhist monk demanding one mantra for enlightenment, laying gold and cloth before him. He tells her it is simple: for nine days, a three-legged white elephant must not enter her dreams, she must not hear a fox howl, and she must not think herself a fool. On the ninth day she sits for pūjā and her mind is full of three-legged white elephants, a howling wolf, and the conviction that she is a fool. Suppression manufactures the thing suppressed. When something keeps returning, that is the signal that a desire is present — and it is placed at the lotus feet of Bhairava to be transmuted, not denied.

Lessons

Drawn already: - Mā is inseparable from Śiva. Śiva without Śakti is a corpse; Śakti without Śiva is movement without ground. The pair is not two things joined but one thing seen from two sides. - The unification of the divine — masculine and feminine as a single reality. - The independence of women. She is not meant to be bound to her partner; her knowledge, her actions, her speech remain her own. - The importance of union in marriage.

Further, from the structure of the two stories:

  • Consent is the precondition of union, not a concession within it. Notice the sequence: she states her terms before she accepts, and he meets them before the marriage. Independence is not a caveat softening the union — the union does not occur without it. This strengthens the independence point rather than sitting beside it.

  • Doubt is the generative act. Kāmeśvara does not pre-exist Brahmā’s question. Nothing manifests until someone articulates the objection out loud. The doubt is not the obstacle the story overcomes; it is the cause of the revelation. Worth holding against the reflex that questioning is an impediment to faith.

  • The radiant form and the cremation ground are the same being. Śiva does not stop being ash-smeared and skull-carrying. Beauty is not the opposite of what Brahmā objected to — it is the same one, under a different question. An argument against sorting reality into acceptable and unacceptable halves.

  • Vātsyāyana’s failure is epistemological, not devotional. He already had darśana of both. Centuries of penance had produced perception without integration. More seeing is not more understanding. And the remedy prescribed for a conceptual deadlock was a practice, not an explanation.

  • Admitting incapacity is a legitimate move. He says outright that he cannot bear the five-faced form, and a bearable form is granted. The tradition meets the practitioner’s actual capacity rather than demanding he withstand what he cannot. This sits well beside the load-calibration principle.

  • The two stories run in opposite directions to the same place. Story 1 is the union descending — a cosmic event with deities as participants. Story 2 is the union being ascended to — a human working twelve years to perceive what has already occurred. Together they argue that the same truth is available from above and from below. That is likely the reason both belong in this chapter rather than one.

  • Desire is routed through, not around. The tradition does not suppress kāma; it transmutes it. The monk story is the proof by contradiction — suppression manufactures obsession. And this is the same claim as the marriage story, delivered as method rather than as myth.

  • The yantra over the mūrti. A striking instruction inside a densely iconographic tradition: do not fix on any single form. It rhymes with the standing instruction to do only the mūla mantra. The chapter can hold that tension rather than resolve it.

What Bhairava Anugraha is

The foundational practice — 144 days

Three maṇḍalas, 48 days each. Minimum 90 to 144. Stated across all three talks as the threshold before anything else opens.

The stages

From the Pañcavaktra talk, which gives the fullest version:

  1. 144 days — the first three maṇḍalas
  2. Prathamācaraṇa — where the bīja-akṣara mantra connections are given. No cost attached. One to three maṇḍalas here.
  3. Bhairava kavaca / Bhairava yantra — rudrākṣa dhāraṇa, 8, 11, and 14 mukhi
  4. Dīkṣā
  5. Tṛtīya — after which the Daśa Mahā mantras begin unlocking

Full span given as 344 days continuous, without break.

This sequence is the structure of the organisation itself, not a general description of tantric practice. It comes directly from the lineage and should be presented as theirs.

On the timescale — two figures, and they are not in conflict: - 344 days is the minimum for the sequence above, done continuously and without break. - A lifetime is what he says it takes to activate one syllable of a mantra, or one Bhairava svarūpa (Part One §12).

The first is a structural requirement. The second is what the structure is for. A book that prints the first without the second is selling a completion date.

Open Questions

Questions to resolve — with the guru, or editorially. The book follows the lineage; these are noted, not resolved here.

  • Mahākāla and Kāla Bhairava both pairing to Kālī. The talk states directly that Kāla Bhairava’s consort is Kālī. Mahākāla is also on the list of ten. If both pair to Kālī, one Mahāvidyā is doubled and one Bhairava is left unpaired. Need the full pairing scheme.

  • Tripurasundarī’s guṇa. The Kāla Bhairava talk places her as sāttvika (with Kālī tāmasika, Tārā rājasika). The Kāmeśvara talk says she carries tāmasika, rājasika and sāttvika together. These may not conflict, but he should say.

  • Dakṣiṇa mārga and Uttara mārga. The Kāmeśvara talk equates them. They are usually treated as distinct streams.

  • The ājñā cakra passage. “We talk about good, bad, and ugly, but unless something is really bothering us, or is really connected to our life, such thoughts will never be generated at all. That only happens through the ājñā cakra.” What exactly is the distinction between the two kinds of thought? And if an active ājñā is the prerequisite for stepping into sādhana, how does one know whether it is active — and what does someone do who finds it is not?

  • Is Kāla Bhairava a manifestation of tamas? He is the guṇa question in miniature. The talk says Śiva is most often in tāmasika and rājasika svarūpa, and that holding the portfolio of destruction is what makes him Rudra, makes him Bhairava. But it never assigns Kāla Bhairava a guṇa outright. Also worth clarifying: the relationship between “Bhairava” as consciousness or tattva and Kāla Bhairava as one named svarūpa among ten — whether the second is a manifestation of the first, and how that language should be used consistently across the book.

  • What is it that accumulates? [EXPAND] From the sañcita passage:

    “You may think that actually one to ten. No — after lakhs of janmas in this existence, we come into the human to make sure that we get to know about the consciousness of the deva who has created us. Even when the creator was not able to understand this, we as humans will always have a fallback — always wondering that I can do anything, I can do anything and everything. Not at all.”

    Three things to open up here:

    1. What is the carrier? Something persists across lakhs of janmas holding the sañcita store. Is that the jīva or the ātman? He uses both words in the same talk — “this jīva does not know where we have come from” — but he also says the ātman is what never dies, and that karma stops when one realises the self beyond doership. If karma stops at the ātman, the ātman cannot be the thing accumulating it. So the jīva looks like the carrier and the ātman the thing beneath it. He never says this outright, and the book should not assert it for him.
    2. Why the human birth specifically? The claim is that after countless lives we arrive here in order to come to know the consciousness of the deva who created us. That makes the human birth purposive rather than incidental — a position with consequences for the rest of the book.
    3. “Even the creator was not able to understand this.” Understand what? That there is a consciousness prior to him? That his own creation contained flaws? The sentence is doing a lot of work and is not unpacked.

  • Bālā or Mahā Tripurasundarī — and is Kāmeśvara the same as Pañcavaktra? This is the sharpest discrepancy so far, and it may not be a discrepancy at all.

    What the Kāmeśvara talk says. The consort is consistently Mahā Tripurasundarī / Lalitā — she manifests, the devatās cannot find her a consort, she marries Kāmeśvara, and the merged consciousness is Kāmeśvarī. At the close he states it plainly: the last one, Kāmeśvara, is connected to Mahā Tripurasundarī. Bālā appears in that talk only as the name of texts — the Bālā Tripurasundarī sahasranāma and khaḍgamālā as the source of the story, the khaḍgamālā hymns recited at the first Śrī Cakra pūjā, and the Bālā stotra Vātsyāyana composed afterward. Never as the consort.

    What the Pañcavaktra talk says. The consort is Bālā Tripurasundarī, whom he also calls Ṣoḍaśī, treating the two as one.

    Why this collides. Both talks identify Kāmeśvara with Pañcavaktra. The Kāmeśvara talk says per the Bhairava Tantra he is shown as Pañcavaktra, and says outright that Pañcavaktra Bhairava and Tripurasundarī once merged are Kāmeśvarī. The Pañcavaktra talk says Sadyojāta — one of the five faces — is himself called Kāmeśvara, paired with Kāmeśvarī.

    So the questions are:

    1. Are Kāmeśvara and Pañcavaktra two chapters or one Bhairava under two names? If one, chapters 8 and 10 are the same figure and the list of ten needs rethinking. If two, what distinguishes them, given that each talk describes the other?
    2. Is Bālā distinct from Mahā Tripurasundarī in this lineage? Conventionally they are distinguished — Bālā is the child form, often described as Lalitā’s daughter, with her own mantra; Ṣoḍaśī is Tripurasundarī herself. The Pañcavaktra talk collapses Bālā and Ṣoḍaśī into one. If the lineage treats all three as one Devī, the pairing holds and there is no conflict. If not, two Bhairavas are drawing on the same Mahāvidyā.

  • How to present Bālā Tripurasundarī’s age. (Editorial, and the most delicate presentational question in the book.)

    The situation. Bālā is conventionally depicted as a nine-year-old girl — bālā means child, and the child form carries nascent, unformed, undiminished power. Your reading of it as purity and innocence is the standard one. But she is simultaneously named as the consort of Pañcavaktra, and the chapter she sits in also contains maithuna among the five offerings. A Western reader encountering “his consort is a nine-year-old” without preparation will not reach for the symbolism. They will reach for something else, and the book will not recover from it.

    What not to do: do not age her up. Describing her as a young woman to avoid the problem misrepresents the tradition, and it fails on its own terms — any reader who later encounters the actual convention will conclude the book was managing them. That is worse than the original difficulty.

    What to ask him:

    1. Is Bālā the consort, or a distinct form? In much of Śrī Vidyā, Bālā is understood as Lalitā’s daughter, not as a spouse — and Ṣoḍaśī is Tripurasundarī herself. He collapses all three. If the lineage distinguishes them, most of this problem dissolves, because the consort is then not the child form.
    2. What does the ninefold child form actually signify in his teaching? Get his words, not a general gloss.
    3. Is the “hidden marriage” language literal-marital or something else? He uses it in a specifically Kaula sense that may not map onto marriage as a reader will hear it.

    How to handle it in the text, once answered: state the convention plainly, give the meaning in the same breath rather than later, and make the Bālā / Ṣoḍaśī / Mahā Tripurasundarī distinction explicit before the word consort is ever used. Do not let a reader assemble the wrong picture and then correct them. This connects directly to the Bālā-vs-Mahā question above — the two need answering together.

  • What is a “hidden marriage tradition”? He says the Pañcavaktra–Ṣoḍaśī pairing is spoken of only in Kaula tantra, never in the Dakṣiṇa mārga or the Veda, and calls it a hidden marriage. The phrase is unfamiliar and the sentence is ambiguous between two quite different claims:

    1. Kaula is, in general, a tradition of hidden marriages — a whole class of concealed consort-pairings that the other streams do not carry. This would be a substantial structural claim about Kaula.
    2. This particular pairing is hidden — i.e. esoteric, transmitted rather than published, and Kaula happens to be the stream that preserves it. A more modest reading, and probably the likelier one.

    Ask which. And if the first: what other hidden marriages are there, what makes a marriage hidden rather than merely esoteric, and hidden from whom — from the other streams, from the uninitiated, or from the deities themselves in some sense? Divine couples are entirely ordinary across these traditions; it is the word “hidden,” and the claim that only one stream carries it, that needs unpacking.

  • Bhairava’s gaze, and how he approaches us (Chapter 3, Stories). Ask Guruji to expand on the gaze in the Atri story — his three eyes looking into Atri rather than at him, piercing into his thoughts and his forgotten purpose (what he came to do), and reflecting back his own pride, fear and ego — and to align it with a teaching on how Bhairava interacts with, or approaches, the sādhaka. In the second Tryambaka talk the guru says Bhairava consciousness makes you aware of who you are and why you are here, and that the question “who am I?” can only be answered with the help of Guru Bhairava. Anna is fairly sure sādhana is how that understanding comes — that the practice is what reveals who you are and why you came into this existence — but wants Guruji to confirm this directly, and to say how he would put it.

  • What is Vidyutāgni, the second fire? (Chapter 3, Guru teachings.) Of the three primordial fires in the second Tryambaka talk, Sūryāgni (the solar fire that purifies karma) and Jaṭharāgni (the digestive fire that turns desire into spiritual fuel) are clear. The second, Vidyutāgni, is not. The guru calls it a lightning fire that awakens consciousness in a flash, links it to vibhūti (the ashes left after lightning), and says it awakened Bharadvāja’s inner [eye] in a single instant — but the caption is garbled through this part (it also renders the name as “vibi agni”), and Anna finds it abstract. Ask Guruji to explain what this fire is, what it does in the sādhaka, and how it relates to vibhūti.

At [49:35] in the second Tryambaka talk the guru says seeking ends only in complete surrender “to the guru tattva — the guru, and the guru tattva inside him, who is trying to teach the entire creation.” Anna marks this as especially important. Ask Guruji which he means, and to expand on how surrender to the guru tattva relates to surrender to the guru.

  • What is “the fire of silence”? (Chapter 3, second Tryambaka talk.) At [55:25] the guru says it is “the fire of the silence” that the ultimate yogī, Tryambaka, was able to teach us, and at [53:41] he calls silence and fire together the essence of tantra. Across both stories silence and fire keep appearing as one thing — Tryambaka born from silence rather than rage, Bharadvāja’s mauna ending in the three fires. Ask Guruji to elaborate: what does he mean by the fire of silence, how can silence burn, and how does a sādhaka come to know it?

  • Why does silence make Akṣobhya so powerful? (Chapter 2, Stories.) In the story of his emergence, the elemental gods (Indra, Vāyu, Agni) are quarrelling over which of them is strongest. Akṣobhya does not enter the argument at all — he simply stands, silent and smiling, unmoved, with no weapons, and watches. Yet one look from him shatters their pride, and they recognise him as the most powerful. Anna finds this especially striking. Ask Guruji to expand on why this makes Akṣobhya Bhairava so powerful: why stillness and non-engagement outrank thunder, wind and fire, and what that teaches the sādhaka.

  • How far does Akṣobhya’s care for the mūlādhāra reach — and how should the book word it? (Chapter 2, Guru teachings.) The guru says that when the ārādhana of Bhairava in the Akṣobhya svarūpa is done, he will take care of mūlādhāra-related problems, naming constipation and menstrual problems [16:38]–[17:31]. Anna reads this as reaching further — sexual issues such as lack of orgasm or premature ejaculation, and conditions like endometriosis or ovarian cancer — possibly to the point of curing them entirely. Ask Guruji: which conditions he means, whether “take care of” means healing, support, or something else, and how he wants it worded. (Editorial: a reader facing a serious illness such as cancer may read a claim of cure as a reason to delay medical care. Anna finds this especially interesting and wants to know the direction of it: does hearing about Bhairava activate samāna, or is it the sādhaka reaching out to him that activates it? Ask Guruji to expand and elaborate on samāna vāyu as a whole, and on this point in particular.

Anna reads this as meaning that certain things have to be happening in the sādhaka’s energy body for Bhairava to come into their sphere of being at all. Ask Guruji to elaborate: what has to be happening in the energy body for this, and how does udāna become active in someone who has not yet heard of him?

  • Can Devī and Bhairava really be activated in tāmasika and rājasika forms — and what happens to the one who does it? (Chapter 2, Guru teachings and Stories.) In the Devānanda story in the Akṣobhya Bhairava talk, the guru says that Devī and Bhairava can be activated in whatever svarūpa — sāttvika, tāmasika, or rājasika — depending on each upāsaka’s will and wish; the tantric who terrorised the village was a sādhaka of Tārā Mā who had activated his tāmasika and rājasika guṇa, with his ego at its peak ([37:03]–[37:37]). Ask Guruji to confirm that this is actually the case, and to expand on it: can the deities truly come into tāmasika or rājasika forms according to the upāsaka’s intent? Anna’s own analysis is that the tantric got what he wanted but suffered for it — he still got wrecked, even though he was using his abilities to harm people — and she thinks that is a lesson worth putting in the book. Ask whether he agrees, and whether misuse always carries a consequence. (Related: the Ekabhatra chapter’s open question on whether boons are morally neutral, from the Madanāsura story.)

  • Why must the sādhaka be grounded in Bhairava before approaching Devī? (Chapter 2, Guru teachings.) In the Akṣobhya Bhairava talk, at [29:24] and again at [47:07], the guru says that unless your grounding in Bhairava is done, even touching any svarūpa of Devī is impossible — it becomes window shopping, a couple of maṇḍalas and then you come back. Anna finds this especially interesting, because many people jump straight into Devī worship — or, worse, Mahāvidyā worship — without worshipping Bhairava first, and without using Bhairava as the way to approach Devī. Ask Guruji to elaborate: why Bhairava has to come first, what happens to someone who goes to the Mahāvidyās without that grounding, and what it means in practice to approach Devī through Bhairava.

  • Are Akṣobhya Bhairava and Akṣobhya Buddha the same? (Chapter 2.) Throughout the Akṣobhya talk the guru moves between the Śaiva Akṣobhya Bhairava and the Akṣobhya of Vajrayāna and Tibetan Buddhism — the Dhyāni Buddha of mirror-like wisdom, lord of Abhirati, protector of the Vajra family, partner of Tārā in the Hevajra Tantra ([25:47], [30:54], [50:45]–[53:58], [54:18]–[57:13]). He shows the two iconographies side by side and closes with “irrespective of whatever tradition … the Akṣobhya svarūpa is one” ([57:55]). Anna first thought they were not the same figure, but notes that in some places he does make it seem that they are the same: he calls the Buddhist figure “Akṣobhya Bhairava” (“In Buddhist tantra, Akṣobhya Bhairava is considered one of the very, very essential [deities],” [30:54]; the Hevajra Tantra’s “divine union of Akṣobhya Bhairava and [Tārā],” [30:04]), and says wherever Akṣobhya is worshipped, in either tradition, “he has been worshipped as Bhairava” ([57:13]). She needs to know for certain before writing this chapter; from here on the outline keeps the two apart, calling the Śaiva figure Akṣobhya Bhairava and the Buddhist one Akṣobhya Buddha. Ask Guruji directly: are they one being seen through two traditions, two distinct figures who share a name and a role, or something else — and how does he want the book to present the Buddhist material?

Production Notes

Decisions about the physical book, tracked separately from content.

  • Mudrā illustrations. Every mudrā named in the book needs a picture. A reader who meets “hold the cin mudrā” and does not already know it cannot act on the sentence, and a wrong guess is worse than none. Currently named: cin mudrā (ch. 8), khecarī mudrā (ch. 8). Expect more as the remaining chapters are drafted. Cin mudrā is conventionally thumb and index fingertip touching, remaining three fingers extended — but this should be drawn from the lineage’s own form, not a stock illustration.

  • Coloured yantras. One per chapter. Decide early whether this means colour plates, inline art, or an appendix — it changes format and printing cost.

  • Iconography plates. Ten Bhairavas, plus possibly two per chapter where a svarūpa has more than one depicted form (see Kāmeśvara: the five-faced form and the radiant form). Note the tension: he explicitly asks people not to indulge in iconography. See Open Questions.

  • Disclaimer placement. The practice disclaimer currently sits in Part Three, after all ten sādhanas. It should also appear in Part One.
