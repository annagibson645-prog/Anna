# A Message Doesn't Have to Be True to Be Believed. It Has to Last.

***Bottom line up front: A message doesn't have to be true to be believed. It has to last. Fear explains part of propaganda, but a message becomes durable through three things: repetition, identity, and social transmission. We believe what we hear again and again, what tells us who we are, and what we repeat to other people. The same machinery works on one person inside a family, a friendship, or a brand. The defense is three questions to ask on the spot: where did I first hear this, what was I feeling, and who am I becoming by repeating it? To trace where one of your own beliefs came from, start with my free Belief Audit. Then test a new behavior with the Archetype Lab. Both are at the end of this piece.***

Someone in my family said everything twice. Then they added the same three words at the end: before you forget.

*Take your coat before you forget. Take your coat before you forget.*

I didn't think much of it. It sounded like care, and maybe it was. But I heard it for years, and somewhere in those years I started to forget things. I'd lose track of a coat, a name, a plan. I told people I had a bad memory, and I said it like a fact I had always known about myself.

I never decided to believe it. Nobody gave me an argument. I just heard it enough times that it became the background music of a huge chunk of my life.

I could have worked on my memory. I tried, now and then. But each attempt came with a quiet sense that I was pushing against something already settled. I was the one who forgets.

It cost me my memory, and it cost me the ability to believe I could remember. I never tried to fix it, because I was the one who forgot. It became an essential part of my identity.

I first doubted the story around 30, six years ago. I was doing introspection, rehashing everything that had happened to me, and I started asking how I could see what my beliefs were and change them.

## Why fear is the lazy answer

So why do we believe what's constantly shoved down our throats? We usually think the answer to this question is fear. This isn't wrong. Fear is a huge part of propaganda. It's also too narrow: a message has to be *durable* before it can be *believed*, and durability can be manufactured. What do I mean by durable?

It has to last, and in order for it to last, the message has to be hammered into us over and over again. The best messages are short, crisp, succinct, and capable of being repeated ad infinitum.

An example is the estate tax, a tax on the inheritance a person leaves behind. Opponents renamed it the "death tax," a phrase popularized by Jack Faris of the National Federation of Independent Business during Newt Gingrich's time as House Speaker. Pollster Frank Luntz found that the name changed opinion. By his polling, about 50 percent of Americans wanted to end the "estate tax," about 60 percent the "inheritance tax," and about 70 percent the "death tax." It was repeated until it entered ordinary speech, and the phrase took off like wildfire.

How many people do you think look at the estate tax as a way to punish the family of the person who died? How deplorable does that sound? Exactly.

## The three mechanisms

### Repetition

The strongest factor, and it needs no emotion: familiarity produces agreement. Fazio et al. 2015 found that knowledge does not protect against illusory truth, so knowing the right answer is not a defense. Pennycook, Cannon & Rand 2018 ran it on real fake-news headlines. The moral-repetition work is the darkest beat: repetition also dulls the sense that an act is wrong.

This is an example on a larger scale.

I listened to the commercials about Abdul El-Sayed in Michigan. He's a Democrat, and he often says things that politicians can easily take out of context. In one commercial, he says "I am dangerous" five times. It is clearly a one-second clip taken from one of his speeches, and it doesn't matter. If you repeat something often enough, other people will repeat it. Soon the entire group will repeat it. I counted how many times they played that clip: five times, one second each. The commercial came on three or four times a day, probably more.

It was harrowing, because now that I understand how influence works, I know what they are doing.

This is what El-Sayed [actually said](https://keyt.com/news/2026/08/12/fact-check-how-tim-scott-and-a-republican-attack-ad-misleadingly-snipped-key-context-from-el-sayeds-dangerous-speech/):

> "I'll just say this. Go ask a child who needed a pair of glasses in Detroit if I'm dangerous. Go ask the folks who could not breathe because of smokestacks spewing out sulfur dioxide if I'm dangerous. Go ask the parents of kids who are going to schools that were leaded if I'm dangerous. Go ask the people flooded in medical debt if I'm dangerous. I think what they'll tell you is: He's the best friend you might have if you want government to work for you. I'll tell you what: If you're AIPAC, I'm pretty damn dangerous. If you're Big Pharma, I'm dangerous. If you're Big Tech and want to put a data center in your backyard, I'm dangerous. If you're a big bank that wants to collude against us, I am dangerous. If you're a monopolist who wants to raise our prices, I'm dangerous. If you're Chuck Schumer, I'm pretty damn dangerous. And I think it's about damn time we had Democrats who are willing to be dangerous for all the people rigging our policies against us."

I am not a Republican or a Democrat. I am just someone who sees how power works in the world and how dangerous it can be to take shit out of context.

How many of you ate that commercial up and ran with it?

Now you know.

### Identity

A message that says *who we are* is a membership card, not an argument. Kahan 2017 covers identity-protective cognition. The beat that carries the section is Kahan, Peters et al. 2012 in *Nature Climate Change*: more science literacy and numeracy made polarization *worse*, not better.

Consider terms like "cult-like brand."

I recently got an Android when I was fresh out of 10 years in iPhone Penitentiary. When I had an iPhone, I literally knew someone who would not date you if she saw your message bubble show up green. She said it was low-status that everyone had an Android, and she didn't need to date someone like that.

That's what I mean by a cult-like brand. If you aren't careful, a brand becomes such a huge part of your identity that you begin to make decisions around it. If you can do that with your phone, what else are you doing that for? Who's holding the lever that controls how you identify?

Picture this. In fourth grade, Maya fails a fractions quiz, and her teacher says, kindly, "Some people just aren't math people." Nobody asks her to agree. She repeats it herself, first as a joke, then as an introduction: "Oh, I'm not a math person." Her friends nod, and it hardens. In high school she skips the harder class. In college she picks a major with no required math. At thirty she hands her tax forms to someone else without opening them. Nobody ever proved the claim. One sentence from someone with authority, repeated for years by Maya herself, decided what she would even try.

### Social transmission

What you repeat to someone else hits harder than what you merely hear. Sharing is partly self-presentation, so the sharer affirms their own position while spreading it. Pressure strengthens a belief rather than loosening it.

Picture this. Your coworker tells you that a colleague is "always late." You aren't sure it's true, but you repeat it to a friend at lunch. The friend says, "Really? I've never noticed." Now you have to defend it, so you name two times it happened. By the end of the week you believe it more firmly than the coworker who told you. You learned nothing new. You said it out loud, and then you were pushed on it.

Your social circle did the rest.

We are often tempted to believe a thing, and that thought is reinforced by the people we hang around. This is where that phrase "you are the sum of the five people you hang around most" comes from. Who you are with shapes who you are, including the things you tell each other.

Honestly, social transmission works more like social contagion.

What beliefs are you sick with?

The reality? Propaganda is the large-scale version of something on a smaller scale that happens in every relationship and within ourselves. Run all three mechanisms against a life the reader recognizes.

- **Repetition:** the family member who has called you the difficult one for years. You absorbed a message, not an argument. Now you think you are 'the black sheep'.
- **Identity:** "that's just how our family is." This is an identity message that makes leaving feel like betrayal.
- **Transmission:** the friend who keeps retelling how difficult you are is building the case, not describing it.

Watch who you hang around, and what messages you absorb.

## Conclusion

Recognition comes first. Once you can see a message being repeated, tied to who you are, or passed from person to person, it stops working quietly.

Then comes deliberate use. The same three properties that make a false message stick can build an honest one. Repetition, identity, and social transmission are not the problem. The line is whether the message tells the truth about something real. Say "I believe in you" to someone often enough and it works the way "before you forget" did, only now it is true, and chosen.

So here is the practical habit: three questions to ask on the spot.

1. Where did I first hear this? Name the person or the place before you name the claim.
2. What was I feeling? Fear, belonging, or just the sound of it for the hundredth time?
3. Who am I becoming by repeating it? Saying it out loud is how it hardens.

You don't need the answers right away, but you damn sure need to start thinking about them. Asking is what interrupts the repetition, and pattern interrupts lead to different behavior. And when you ask those questions, you are farther along than you think.
